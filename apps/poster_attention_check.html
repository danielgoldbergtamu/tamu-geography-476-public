Poster Attention Check

Upload your poster, draw a box around each map, legend, picture and block of text, and see where a visitor's eyes are likely to go first. Then check it in grayscale, for color-blind readers, and from six feet away.

Export your poster from PowerPoint or Google Slides as a PNG (best) or a PDF, then choose the file here. Only the first page of a PDF is used. The poster stays on your computer; nothing is uploaded to a server.

Click, then drag a box around each map on your poster, including small inset maps. The page cannot recognize a map by itself. Marking it tells the page where your maps are, so it gives them the attention a geographer gives a map. Click the button again to stop drawing.
?
Click, then drag a box around each legend or map key. Someone with GIS basics and a GIS expert check the legend to learn what the colors mean, so the visitor views use these boxes.
?
Click, then drag a box around each photo, drawing, chart, logo or icon. A newcomer who knows no geography looks at these first.
?
Click, then drag a box around each block of text: introduction, data and method, results, conclusions and references. A GIS expert reads the method and results text to decide whether to trust the map. Do not box the title; the page finds big type near the top on its own.
?
Removes every box you drew so you can start over. It does not remove your poster.
See it as
?
Everyone: the general attention model. Newcomer: knows no geography; reads the title, looks at pictures and bright color, skips legends and methods. GIS basics: has taken a GIS course; goes to the map first, then the legend, scale and title. GIS expert: a GIS professional; reads the map, legend, method and results, and asks whether to trust the map. Mark your maps, legends, pictures and text first, or the visitor views have little to go on.

Everyone: the general attention model. Pick a visitor to see how a newcomer, someone with GIS basics, or a GIS expert reads the same poster.

?
Attention: the heatmap. Red is looked at longest; the numbers are the likely reading order. Original: your poster with your boxes. Grayscale: a black-and-white printer or projector. Red-green and Blue-yellow: how a reader with that kind of color blindness sees it; about 1 in 12 men has red-green color blindness. From six feet: roughly what reads from across the aisle at GIS Day.

Likely reading order

    Check your poster

    • Is your map number 1 or 2? If not, make it bigger or give it more contrast.
    • Switch between the three visitors. Does the newcomer find your finding? Does the expert find your method and legend?
    • Does the title get looked at? It should be readable from six feet.
    • Is something unimportant pulling eyes, like a logo, a photo or a bright color block?
    • In grayscale and the color-blind views, can you still tell the map classes apart?
    • In the six-foot view, can you still read the title and your main finding?

    This predicts attention from contrast, size, position and the boxes you mark. The visitor views use the same weights as the three kinds of visitor in the posters lecture. It is a model, not real eye tracking. Where you disagree with it, be ready to say why.
