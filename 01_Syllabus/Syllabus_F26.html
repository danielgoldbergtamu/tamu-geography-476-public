Catalog Description

This course is the capstone learning experience of the Department of Geography’s Geographic Information Science & Technology (GIST) major. Through seminar style discussions course participants are introduced to current topics in the spatial sciences profession. An accompanying independent research project will build professional research skills. The course introduces students to professional ethical issues surrounding spatial technologies and information. Professional presentation of scientific and technical results will be taught and practiced. The course will also introduce students to GIST certification activities and help them to construct a professional GIST portfolio.

 

Course Description

Introduction to current topics in Geographic Information Science including ethical and legal issues surrounding spatial technologies, proper GIS management practices and professional certification. Development of professional research, technical and communication skills through participation in a coordinated internship or independent research project.

 

Course Prerequisites

GEOG and GIST majors; sophomore classification or approval of instructor. No other Prerequisites.

 

Special Course Designation

W-Course Designation

This class is a W (writing intensive) class. See the "Assignment Types and Weights" section below as well as the  "Best Practices for W & C Courses"  for grading details including what students will have to write and speak to earn credit for the course.

W-Course Grading Requirements

Grading for this course will be based on completion of the University "W" course requirements. 35% of the graded assignments for the course will include individual writing assignments totaling at least 2,000 words of finished written materials. In addition, another 45% of the course assignments will be individual writing assignments totaling at least 2,000 words of finished written materials which will be complemented with a combination of oral presentation slides, handouts, scripts, web sites, and posters which accompany any oral presentation (5 minutes). The "public speaking" component of the course will be completed by individual students live, in person. Student assignments (individual and group) will be provided with formative feedback including review and assessment by the instructor as well as peer-review by fellow students.

To pass this course, students must pass the Writing (W) component.

 

Course Learning Outcomes

The intent of this class is to provide students with a professionalizing academic experience. This will be accomplished in two ways. First a set of structured discussions and projects will introduce students to topics that will affect them in their professional careers as well as to develop their oral, written and cartographic communication skills. Secondly, an independent research project will provide students an opportunity to apply the geospatial skills they have learned in previous classes to an individual project. The progress on the project will be articulated in writing, through oral presentations and by the creation of maps and other graphics.

At the end of this class, each student will be able to:

  1. Explain current ethical and legal issues surrounding Geospatial technologies; and
  2. Articulate the current state of professional certification in the Geospatial arena and how obtaining professional certification may affect career opportunities; and
  3. Define and describe current national and international standards applicable to spatial data; and
  4. Apply appropriate GIS analytical techniques to successfully solve a research problem; and
  5. Communicate the methods used and results achieved in an independent research project via written, oral and cartographic methods.

Artificial Intelligence Statement

You are required to use artificial intelligence in this course, and you are graded on how well you direct and evaluate it — never on whether you used it. This is a deliberate design decision and it applies to the work that carries the marks, not to an optional extra assignment.

The reason is the job you are about to apply for. Employers in geospatial work increasingly assume a candidate can put these tools to work on code, analysis, documentation and writing, and can say when the output is wrong. The failure mode of these tools is not incompetence, it is confidence: they produce fluent, well-formatted, plausible answers about GIS methods they have gotten wrong. In a classroom exercise that costs you a grade. In work a client acts on, it costs something else. Catching that is a professional skill in short supply, and it cannot be taught in the abstract — it has to be practiced on work that matters, which is what this course has.

What is assessed is your judgment, not your usage. You are assessed on whether you asked a demanding question rather than a vague one, whether you noticed what the answer assumed, whether you verified a method before relying on it, and above all what you rejected and why. You are not assessed on which tool you chose, on how much of the work the tool did, or on whether the output was impressive. Accepting everything an assistant produces is not review, it is transcription, and it is visible in a log.

Most graded work carries an AI Review Log, an AI Method Log, or both. These are part of the deliverable, not paperwork attached to it: an otherwise strong submission with an empty or invented log is not a complete submission. The instruments do not change across the semester — what rises is the standard for completing them well.

You own everything you submit. Every error, fabricated citation, invented function, mis-stated projection and hallucinated data source is yours once it is in your work, exactly as if you had written it. The tool is not a co-author and cannot be cited as an excuse.

Undisclosed use is an academic integrity violation, and so is a log that describes work that did not happen. The requirement is disclosure and documentation, not permission — you never have to ask whether you may use a tool on the work of this course. The Aggie Honor Code applies here as it does everywhere else.

Client material goes through university-approved tools and nowhere else. The projects in this course are real problems brought by outside organizations. Data, documents and anything else they provide stay inside the TAMU platform; they are not pasted into a personal account of a consumer service.

Your AI work is assessed under a piloted course-level outcome, AI-C, which is being taught and assessed this year to establish whether it belongs in the approved course description. It is course-level only and it is labeled as piloted everywhere it appears, including on the assignments that carry it, so that nothing you are graded against is invisible to you.

The full version of this — what the logs ask for, how the expectation rises through the term, worked examples, and the reviewer personas you will be asked to use — is on the AI Usage in this Class page in Canvas, which is required reading in week one.
