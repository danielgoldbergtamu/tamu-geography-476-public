How the session runs

The question today refuses to let you avoid

How do I know this is correct?

Two answers do not count, and both are common. "It ran" is a statement about your software. "The numbers look plausible" is a statement about your expectations — and your expectations are the thing most likely to be wrong, because you built the method that produced the numbers.

Three answers do count.

  • Ground truth. Compare against something independently known to be true for a sample: field observation, an authoritative count, a published figure produced by somebody who is not you.
  • A second method. Get the same answer a different way. If two methods that share no assumptions agree, the agreement is evidence; if they disagree, you have learned something more useful than either result.
  • Sensitivity. Change the parameters you chose — the threshold, the buffer, the classification break, the unit of aggregation — and see whether the finding survives. A finding that only exists at one buffer distance is a finding about the buffer.

And a fourth answer, which is honest rather than weak: this cannot be validated, and here is precisely why. Write down what would be needed and why it is not available. That is a finding the client can act on, and it belongs in the report rather than in a silence.

The source you stopped checking

You have spent this session asking whether your result is right. Here is the question almost nobody asks: when did you last check whether your tool is?

In A Spy’s Guide to Running AI (not included in the public copy — a third-party document, shared with this class under a permission that does not extend to redistribution), three former intelligence officers — John O’Neil, Jim Lawler and Mike Mears — argue that a generative model should be run the way a case officer runs a human source: useful, fast, sometimes brilliant, sometimes wrong, and never a substitute for judgment. The part of that worth six minutes of a validation session is the part they say does not exist yet in the literature: when do you stop?

Their four conditions, translated to what you are doing:

  • Its known failure modes keep landing on your critical questions. A model that is unreliable about projections is fine for drafting and bad for the part of your method that depends on projections. The problem is not the model; it is using it where it fails.
  • It was consequentially wrong once and you never worked out why. Continuing at the same level of trust after that is not generosity, it is an unexamined decision.
  • It was updated. "Treat it as a new source and revalidate. Prior reliability does not transfer automatically."
  • You have been leading the witness. If your prompts have been shaping the answers, the agreement you have been getting is about you.

The third one is the one that will actually happen to you. Field 1 of the AI Review Log asks for the tool and the model version — TAMU AI Chat (Claude Sonnet 4.5), not "an AI". That field has looked like bookkeeping all semester. It is not: the model you are relying on in November is not the one you calibrated against in August, and nothing announced the change. The reason field 1 asks for a version is so that you can notice.

So: write the version down today. Then write one sentence saying what would make you stop using it for a specific part of your work. Not "if it gets things wrong" — a condition specific enough that you would recognize it when it happened.

What the room learned about its own confidence

Before you run your validation, write down a single number: how confident are you, from 0 to 10, that your result is right? Write it where you can see it later. After you have run it, write the number again.

In the last six minutes the room reads out the pair — "seven, then four" — and nothing else. No explanations yet.

Then two questions, in this order.

  • Which direction did the room move? It usually moves down, and the size of the drop is the value of the exercise. You are not being marked on confidence; you are learning how much of yours was unearned.
  • Whose number did not move, and what did they run? This is the one worth listening for. A validation that could not have failed is not a validation — checking that your output has the number of rows you expected, or re-running the same method on the same data, tests nothing. If your confidence was unchanged, look hard at whether the test you chose had any way of coming out badly.

Take the number you end with into your next draft. A result reported with the confidence it actually earned is worth more here, and to a client, than one reported with the confidence you wanted it to have.

Review each other's work, here, now

12 minutes, in class, and it replaces a Canvas peer review. Swap with the person next to you. Read theirs, then talk — do not write a document. You are reviewing Project Draft 2, which was due on Tuesday.

What to look for

  • Could you re-run this method from the description alone? Where would you get stuck?
  • Is the pilot output actually shown, or only claimed?
  • Does the Status Report include problems, or only progress?

No written review is submitted for this. What is submitted is what you do next: the change you make because of what you heard, recorded in your AI Method Log or your next draft. Feedback you did not act on is feedback you did not get — and the person who told you it was wrong is sitting next to you, so ask them.
