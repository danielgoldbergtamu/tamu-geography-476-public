How the session runs

Three checks that count, and one honest answer

How do you know your result is correct? Two answers do not count. "It ran" is a statement about your software. "The numbers look plausible" is a statement about your expectations, and your expectations are the thing most likely to be wrong, because you built the method that produced the numbers.

Three answers do count. Pick the one you can run today:

  • Ground truth. Compare a sample against something known to be true: a field observation, an authoritative count, or a published figure made by somebody who is not you.
  • A second method. Get the same answer a different way. If two methods that share no assumptions agree, the agreement is evidence. If they disagree, you have learned something more useful than either result.
  • Sensitivity. Change a number you chose, such as the threshold, the buffer or the unit of aggregation, and see whether the finding survives. A finding that only exists at one buffer distance is a finding about the buffer.

A fourth answer is honest rather than weak: this cannot be checked, and here is exactly why. Say what would be needed and why it is not available. That is a finding the client can act on.

In class, the check is part of the pair activity. Your partner asks which check would test your result and what result would prove it wrong. A check that disagrees with your result is a result, not a failure. Run the check before Project Draft 2 is due, and put what it found in the draft.

Establishes, suggests, is consistent with, does not show

These four are not degrees of politeness. They are four different claims about evidence, and choosing the right one is the interpretation work — most of what separates a professional finding from a student result is knowing which of these you have earned.

Establishes, suggests, is consistent with, does not show
The phraseWhat it claimsWhat it costs you to say it
EstablishesThe evidence here is sufficient. Somebody who disagrees has to attack the methodValidation, a stated uncertainty, and a result that survived somebody trying to break it
SuggestsThe evidence points this way and is not sufficient on its ownYou must say what would settle it — otherwise this is a hedge rather than a claim
Is consistent withThe evidence does not contradict this. It is also consistent with other explanationsYou have to name at least one of those other explanations, or the phrase is doing dishonest work
Does not showStating the limit of your own result, on purposeNothing. It is the cheapest sentence you will write and the one that protects everything else

Causal words are where this goes wrong. Drives, leads to, because of, causes, results in, explains — each asserts that changing one thing changes the other, which correlation cannot show on its own. Watch for these in the pair activity.

Defend your result to your partner

Work in pairs. Your partner plays the skeptical client, and then you switch.

  1. Read your partner the sentence in your Project Draft 2 file that states your main finding.
  2. Your partner asks which check would test it, and what result would prove it wrong.
  3. Your partner picks the verb you have earned and flags any causal word you did not show.
  4. Your partner asks what your result does not establish, and which source of bias could explain it.

Then I will call on pairs to report back: which claim your partner pushed back on, which verb you ended up with, and which gaps are worth telling the client. Fix your claim and your gap in Project Draft 2.

Write your team's statement

Work with your team in your team's shared document. Your client hears one finding from the team, not five from its members.

  1. Write three sentences on what the team now knows that it did not know in August. Start from each member's three sentences.
  2. List where components support each other and where they disagree. Put the disagreements in, not only the agreements.
  3. List the team's information gaps. They are a headline finding for the client, not an appendix.
  4. Run your three sentences through the Overclaim Detector in TAMU AI Chat. As a team, accept or reject each flag, and fix the sentences for each flag you accept.

Then I will call on one person from each team to read the statement. We will discuss where the components disagree and which one is wrong, which flags the team rejected and why, and which gaps belong at the top of the report. The statement goes in Project Draft 2.

Judge the Overclaim Detector's flags on your statement

The persona is in the AI Reviewer Persona Catalog. Paste your team's three sentences and it returns every sentence it believes claims more than the evidence supports, with a rewrite.

Judging the flags is the graded part, not collecting them. Expect it to be roughly right and specifically wrong, in three predictable ways.

  • It flags hedged sentences anyway. A sentence that already says suggests gets rewritten to say may possibly suggest. Reject that one and say why: the claim already matched the evidence, and vagueness is not accuracy.
  • It cannot see your check. It only has the paragraph, so a claim you actually earned with today's check looks unearned to it. Reject it, and note that the fix is in your paragraph rather than in your result: if the reviewer cannot tell you checked it, neither can the client.
  • It misreads domain language. A technical term with a precise meaning gets read as a strong general claim. Reject it, and name the term.

Then act on the ones that are right, which there will be. Each of you records the whole exchange in your own AI Review Log — what it flagged, what you accepted, and what you rejected with the reason. Field 5, what you rejected and why, is the field that carries the points.

Three flags rejected with good reasons is a better log than ten accepted without any.
