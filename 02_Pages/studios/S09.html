How the session runs

Four choices to defend on your map

Someone at GIS Day will ask about each of these. Have an answer ready for every one.

  • Projection. Does it keep the areas or the distances your argument depends on? A web map projection inflates area toward the poles.
  • Classification. Equal interval, quantiles or natural breaks. If another choice would change the story, say which one you chose and why.
  • Color. Sequential for more and less, diverging around a meaningful middle value, and distinct hues for categories.
  • Normalization. Counts mostly show where people live. Rates and percentages show the pattern.

What goes on today's draft

A rough draft is enough, and placeholder text is fine:

  • Your map, in the middle and the largest thing on the poster.
  • A title that states your finding and reads from six feet.
  • A caption: one sentence under the map that says what it shows.
  • Your finding in three short lines, not a paragraph.
  • At the bottom: your name, your client and your sources.

Make your poster draft

Work on your own laptop while I walk around. Open a new slide in PowerPoint or Google Slides, sized for the GIS Day poster. Put your one map in the middle and make it the largest thing on the poster. Add a title that states your finding, a one-sentence caption under the map, your finding in three short lines, and your name, client and sources at the bottom. Use the map you already have; do not make a new one today. When I call time, export the slide as a PNG or PDF. This draft becomes your Project Poster.

Open the Poster Attention Check

Scan the QR code on the slide, or open the Poster Attention Check (claude.ai/artifact/UW9X1Z1vNhskBdmtRzJmUv). The page runs in your browser, and your poster is not uploaded anywhere. It uses the same predicted-attention model as the heatmaps on last year's GIS Day posters in the posters lecture.

Marking boxes tells the page what each part of your poster is. A contrast model cannot recognize a map. When you mark your maps, the page gives them the extra attention a geographer gives a map. When you also mark legends, pictures and text blocks, the three visitor buttons can weight them the way each visitor reads: a newcomer looks at pictures and bright color, someone with GIS basics goes to the map and legend, and a GIS expert reads the map, legend and the method and results text.

Run the attention check on your draft

  1. Upload your exported poster.
  2. Mark every map, legend, picture and text block, using the Mark buttons and dragging a box around each one.
  3. Look at the reading order. Is your map number 1 or 2? If not, make it bigger or give it more contrast.
  4. Click Newcomer, GIS basics and GIS expert. Does the newcomer find your finding? Does the expert find your legend and method?
  5. Click Grayscale, the three color-blind views and From six feet. Can you still tell the map classes apart, and can you still read the title?

The page predicts attention from contrast, size, position and the boxes you mark. It is a model, not real eye tracking, so judge it: if you disagree with it, be ready to say why. Bring one problem on your poster to the class review, and keep a screenshot of the heatmap for Engagement 7.

Ask AI to be three poster visitors

The posters lecture showed three kinds of visitor reading the same poster with different questions. Now you ask AI to be each of them, using your own poster and its heatmap.

  1. In TAMU AI Chat, start a new chat and attach two images: your poster as a PNG and a screenshot of its heatmap from the Poster Attention Check.
  2. Paste the Newcomer Visitor prompt from the AI Persona Catalog. The same prompts are in the AI Prompt Library.
  3. In two more new chats, run the GIS Basics Visitor and GIS Expert Visitor prompts with the same two images.
  4. Compare each visitor's first three stops with the heatmap's numbers.

The AI is guessing too. Where it disagrees with the heatmap, decide which one is right and why. Log the three answers, and what you rejected, in your AI Review Log.

The prompt for each visitor

Each prompt starts with one line that sets up the visitor:

  • Newcomer. You know no geography. You read the title and look at pictures and bright color.
  • GIS basics. You have taken one GIS course. You go to the map, then the legend and title.
  • Expert. You are a GIS professional. You read the map and method, and ask if you trust it.

The rest of the prompt is the same for all three:

I attached my poster and a heatmap of where people are likely to look. Red means looked at longest. The numbers are the reading order. In 30 seconds, where do your eyes go first, second and third? Where do you disagree with the heatmap? What is the finding, what did you skip, and what one change would help you most?

The full prompts, ready to paste, are under Presentations in the AI Persona Catalog.

Review posters as a class

I will call on you one at a time. Open your poster and its heatmap on the room computer. Where did the check say eyes go first, and do you agree? What broke in grayscale, the color-blind views or from six feet? We will discuss the one change that would help your poster most. Two minutes each, and be specific, such as "the legend is too small to read." If I do not get to you, look for the same problems on your own poster.

Fix it, then write the caption

Make the one change the review or the check pointed to, then run the attention check again and compare the two heatmaps. Under your map, put one sentence that says what the map shows. If that sentence is hard to get right, fix the map, not the sentence. "Median income by county" is a label. "Income falls with distance from the city" is a caption.

Make your team's posters match

Put your team's posters side by side on one laptop. I will call on one person from each team: which colors, fonts and map elements do you share, what does not match yet, and who changes it? At GIS Day, posters that match look like one project, which they are.

Review each other's work, here, now

11 minutes, in class, and it replaces a Canvas peer review. Swap with the person next to you. Read theirs, then talk — do not write a document. You are reviewing Project Draft 3, which was due on Tuesday.

What to look for

  • Is there real validation evidence, or is validation only mentioned?
  • Are edge cases and failures documented, or has the analysis been presented as smoother than it was?
  • Is any claim stronger than the evidence shown supports?

No written review is submitted for this. What is submitted is what you do next: the change you make because of what you heard, recorded in your AI Method Log or your next draft. Feedback you did not act on is feedback you did not get — and the person who told you it was wrong is sitting next to you, so ask them.

AI use required on this assignment

Using AI on this assignment is required, not optional. You are being assessed on how well you direct and evaluate the tool — the skill employers now assume you have.
