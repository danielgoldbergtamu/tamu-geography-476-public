How the session runs

What leaves this room today is documentation a stranger can run. Not notes you can follow — notes somebody who was not here, and who cannot ask you anything, can follow to the same numbers. That is the standard, and the only way to find out whether you have met it is to watch somebody try.

What reproducible means here, and the three things that always break it

Reproducible in this course means one thing, and it is narrower than the word usually is: another person, on another machine, working from your written method and the data you name, gets the same result. Not a similar result. The same one.

That deliberately leaves out tidiness. You can be organized, consistent and version-controlled and still fail this test, because what breaks reproducibility is almost never disorder. It is three things, and it is nearly always one of these three rather than something exotic.

What reproducible means here, and the three things that always break it
What breaks itWhat it looks likeWhat to write instead
A path that only exists on your machineA downloads folder, a network drive nobody else is on, a layer already open in your projectThe data source by name and vintage, and where a teammate can obtain it
A parameter you set once and forgotA buffer distance, a coordinate reference system, a threshold, a classification break, a random seed — chosen in a dialog weeks ago and never written downEvery number, with its unit, and why it has that value rather than another
A version you never named"ArcGIS Pro", "the census data", "the road network" — each of which was several different things this yearTool and version, data set and vintage, and the date you obtained it

The pattern is the same in all three: the step is invisible to you because it is already true on your machine. That is why rereading your own documentation does not find these, and why the swap is the only test that works.

The swap — fourteen minutes, and one rule

  1. Save your documentation in the team's shared folder and tell your teammate its file name. The documentation only. Not your screen, not your project file, not one sentence of context. If your team has no shared folder, the Project Manager creates one before the swap starts.
  2. They find the step 1 input data on their own laptop, from your document alone, then walk every other step on paper. For each step they write what they would click or type, and mark every place they would have to ask you. They do what it says, not what they can tell you meant.
  3. You say nothing. That is the rule, it is the whole exercise, and it is harder than it sounds. Write down where they stopped instead of answering it.
  4. Swap back, and do the same for them.

Record the place they stopped, not the reason you would have given. "They could not find the input" is an observation. "They should have known it was on the shared drive" is you supplying the missing step out loud — and it is exactly the sentence that will not be there in November.

Where people got stuck, read out

Each pair reads out one place their reader stopped. One line each. The room is listening for a pattern rather than for individual mistakes, and there is one to hear.

Two questions, in this order.

  • Which of the three was it — a path, a parameter, or a version? Nearly every stop is one of them. Hearing eight in a row is how you learn to recognize your own.
  • Would you have found it by rereading your own method? The answer is almost always no, and that is what the exercise reveals about your own documentation: it reads as complete to you because you supply the missing steps without noticing that you are supplying them.

The nine minutes after this are for fixing what your reader stumbled on, while you can still remember what you meant.
