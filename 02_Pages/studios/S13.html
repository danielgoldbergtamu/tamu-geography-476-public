        How the session runs

        What this session is

        This is the last working session of the course, and it is a working session — nothing here is delivered at you. You arrive with pieces and you leave with one document.

        On your own. Assemble your component's section: what you did, what you found, what you could not determine. Write your handoff — where the difficulties are, what you would do differently, what you tried that did not work. Confirm your methods documentation still describes what you actually did, which after a semester of changes is rarely still true.

        As a team. Integrate the sections into one report with a single argument rather than five stapled together. Find where two sections contradict each other and decide which one is wrong — publishing both is not an option, and discovering it after the client does is worse. Write the information gaps section, which is the part the client and the next cohort will use most. The PM writes the post-mortem.

        The contradiction hunt is the part people skip

        Five people wrote five sections against the same data and did not read each other's. Somewhere in there, two of you state something incompatible — a figure that disagrees with a number in the text, a limitation one of you claims and another ignores, two different accounts of what the data can support.

        Find it now. A reviewer who finds it for you has stopped believing the rest of the document, and a client who finds it after acting on the report has a worse problem than that.

        The debrief nobody runs

        Field 5 of the AI Review Log has asked you, on every submission since week one, what you rejected and why. Nothing has ever asked whether you were right to.

        That gap is not an oversight peculiar to this course. In A Spy's Guide to Running AI (not included in the public copy — a third-party document, shared with this class under a permission that does not extend to redistribution), John O'Neil, Jim Lawler and Mike Mears set out the protocol a case officer follows after every meeting with a source, and it has one step this course's log does not: Review — what happened after we acted? Did the analysis hold up? They are blunt that it is the step organizations skip, and blunt about the cost: a source you never debrief after the fact is one whose reliability you can never actually assess.

        You are now in the only week of the semester where you can run it. You have fifteen weeks of logs and a finished component, so you can see how the story ended.

        Five minutes. Go back through your own logs and find two things.

        • A critique you rejected that you should have accepted. It exists. Somewhere a reviewer flagged something, you had a reason, and the reason turned out to be wrong — the claim did not survive validation, the reader you dismissed was the client, the term you defended did confuse people. Write down what it cost you: a rewrite in week ten, a figure remade, a finding walked back.
        • A critique you accepted that you should have rejected. Rarer, and more interesting. A place where a fluent, confident objection sent you to fix something that was not broken.

        Then one sentence, and it goes in your report's AI accounting: what your rejections were systematically wrong about. Not a list — a pattern. Overruling the tool on domain conventions and being right; overruling it on how a non-specialist reads a sentence and being wrong. That sentence is the closest thing you will produce to a calibration of your own judgment, which is the thing the whole instrument was built to develop.

        What a report section owes a reader who was not in the room

        Everyone who reads this report was not in the room. The client was not in your studio, the next cohort was not in this semester, and in four months neither were you — you will have forgotten which of your own numbers came from which run.

        A section owes that reader five things, and it fails as a professional document if any one of them is missing.

        What a report section owes a reader who was not in the room
        What it owesThe testWhat failing it looks like
        What you didSomebody could re-run it from this section and the documentation it points at"We analyzed the data using standard methods"
        What you foundThe finding is stated as a sentence, with a number in it, before any discussion of itA figure with a caption, and the reader left to work out what it shows
        What you could not resolveThe limits are stated by you rather than discovered by the readerSilence, which the reader correctly reads as a claim that there are none
        How confident you are, and whyEvery claim carries the word it earned — establishes, suggests, is consistent withOne register throughout, usually the strongest one
        Where the next person startsSomebody could continue this without a conversation with you"Future work could explore this further"

        The last one is the one that gets written badly, because it is written last and it feels like housekeeping. It is not. This report goes to the client and to the students who pick the work up next year — your handoff is what decides whether they build on your component or start it again from nothing.

        Write it for a specific person: a student like you, in August, who has your files and cannot ask you anything. Tell them where the difficulties are, what you would do differently, and what you tried that did not work — that last one is the most valuable paragraph in the document and the one nobody writes, because it is the only place a reader learns which roads are closed.

        AI use required on this assignment

        Using AI on this assignment is required, not optional. You are being assessed on how well you direct and evaluate the tool — the skill employers now assume you have.

        2. Read what it returns critically. Note anything it gets wrong about your component — that is the part worth recording.
        3. Record what you ran and what you did with it. This feeds the AI Review Log on whichever assignment this studio is building toward.
