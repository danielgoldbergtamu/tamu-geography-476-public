You are required to use AI in this course, and you are graded on how well you direct and evaluate it — never on whether you used one. This page explains what that means in practice, why it is here, and where the limits are. Everything else about AI in this course is an application of what is on this page.

This page is kept current, so read it more than once. Where a clarification or a change is needed as the course goes on, it is made here first and you are told about it. If an assignment and this page ever disagree, this page is the one that governs, and you should say so.

The short version

  • The work comes first. You spend the semester on a real problem for an outside client. AI is how a lot of that work gets done, the way a projection or a version-control system is how it gets done. It is a tool in service of the deliverable.
  • You must use it, and you must show your work. Most graded work carries an AI Review Log or an AI Method Log, or both.
  • You are assessed on judgment, not usage. The field that carries the most weight is the one where you say what the tool got wrong and what you did instead.
  • Accepting everything is not review. It is transcription, and it is visible in a log.
  • The writing you submit is your own. This is a W course: the credit is for your writing, so the words come from you first, typed or dictated. AI may then help you refine them, and it goes to work on everything around them: sources, checks, and arguing with what you wrote.
  • The boundary is the university platform. Client material goes through TAMU-approved tools and nowhere else.

Why this is in a GIS capstone

Two things have changed in the market you are about to enter, and this course is built around both.

Employers now expect it. Job descriptions in geospatial work increasingly assume a candidate can put these tools to work — on code, on documentation, on analysis, on writing — and can say when the output is wrong. That expectation arrived faster than curricula did. A graduate who can show a semester of documented, critical AI use in professional work has something specific to talk about in an interview; one who can only say "I have used it" does not.

The failure mode is confidence, not incompetence. These tools produce fluent, well-formatted, plausible answers about GIS methods they have got wrong. In a classroom exercise that costs you a mark. In work a client acts on, it costs something else. Learning to catch that is a professional skill with a short supply, and it cannot be taught in the abstract — it has to be practiced on work that matters, which is what this course has.

What you should leave with. Not familiarity with one product, which will be obsolete. The habit of directing a capable, unreliable assistant and verifying what comes back — which transfers to whatever replaces it.

Where the boundaries are

There are two rules, and which one applies depends on the assignment. This is the thing to get straight before anything else, because the course means both of them.

On the W assignments — the Certification Essay, the Ethics Essay, the Resume, the Portfolio and the Technical Presentation — the draft is yours. GEOG 476 is a writing-intensive course, and a W course's credit is for the student's own writing — 2,000 words of it, with a draft, feedback and a revision. Those assignments are where the 2,000 words come from, so the words you hand in are words you wrote. You may type them or dictate them, and dictation counts as your own words. Once the words are yours, AI may help you refine them: tighten a sentence, fix grammar, or suggest a clearer phrasing that you accept or change. Reading AI-generated text aloud into a dictation tool does not make it your words. Everything else the tool is good for is not only allowed but required: finding sources, checking a claim, arguing against your position, telling you which paragraph does not do what you think it does. What is assessed is what you did with the argument you got back, and the log is where you show it.

On the project sequence, where the first draft comes from does not matter. The pitch, the three drafts, the poster and the final report sit on top of the W requirement and count toward none of it. You may write those drafts or have AI generate them. That is not a lowering of the standard, it is a different standard: project work is judged the way professional work is judged — is it right, and can you defend it to someone who asks. A client does not care who typed the sentence; they care whether the finding holds.

Everything else in the course is not a W component at all — attendance, the engagements, the GIS Day items. The W rules do not reach them.

Use the university platform. TAMU's AI tools are contracted so that your prompts are not used to train a vendor's model, and they are approved for university data. Consumer tools are not, and what you paste into one leaves the university.

Client material stays inside that boundary. The problems in this course come from real organizations. Their briefs, their data and your analysis of it go through approved tools only. If you are unsure whether something counts, ask before you paste it — not after.

Disclosure is the design, not a penalty. The logs are how you disclose, they are graded work, and using AI without logging it is the thing that is actually a problem. This course does not ask you to hide the tool; it asks you to account for it.

The Aggie Honor Code still applies. Submitting work you cannot explain, defend, or reproduce is a violation whether a person or a tool produced it. Every deliverable in this course is one you will be asked to stand behind in front of people who ask hard questions.

Two things you do on every AI conversation in this course

These do not depend on which rule applies. They apply the first time you open a tool for coursework and every time after, on W assignments, on project work and on everything else.

  1. Announce yourself before anything else. Open with a prompt that says who you are, what the assignment is, and whether you may use AI to generate words you turn in. The exact sentence is on each assignment page, because the middle of it changes with the assignment. Ask the model to restate the rule in its own words before you start - a model that has misread it shows you on the first turn, which is worth more than a model that simply agrees.
  2. Keep the transcript and hand it in. Save the whole conversation and submit it with the assignment. The AI Review Log is what you claim; the transcript is the evidence for it. Where the log says which critiques you rejected, the transcript shows what you were actually offered - which is the part that cannot be reconstructed afterward.

Neither of these is a trap. The announcement exists because a model that has been told the rule behaves differently from one that has not, and you should see that difference for yourself. The transcript exists because disclosure is the design of this course: the thing that is a problem here is an unlogged use, not a logged one.

What AI may and may not do, item by item

The section above is the reasoning. This is the list. It is the same list the department has been given for evaluating this course, written here in the terms you will actually meet it in — and if something you want to do is not on either list, ask before you do it rather than after.

#### On the W assignments — W Writing, W Speaking

These are the assignments that earn the W designation and carry the 2,000 words. The rule is the strict one: you write the draft.

The draft itself

AI may

  • Find and summarize sources before you write, and say where each one came from
  • Check a claim you have already written against something outside the model
  • Critique your draft: what is unsupported, what does not follow, what a reader will object to
  • Argue the opposite of your position, as hard as it can be argued
  • Read the assignment back to you and say what it is actually asking for
  • Help you refine words you wrote first: tighten a sentence, fix grammar, or suggest a clearer phrasing that you then accept or change

AI may not

  • Write the draft, or any passage of it, that you then submit
  • Replace your draft with a version written in its own words
  • Produce an outline that you then fill in without changing
  • Generate the words you hand in, under any arrangement of prompting

Feedback and revision

AI may

  • Give you a round of critique before any human sees the work, as often as you like, at any hour
  • Tell you which parts of your instructor's feedback you have not yet acted on
  • Point at the exact sentence a critique is about

AI may not

  • Replace the feedback your instructor gives you
  • Do the revising for you. A revision you did not make is not a revision
  • Be asked first, before you have formed your own view of your draft

AI as something you are learning to direct

AI may

  • Be the thing you practice against: a reviewer you can argue with as often as you like
  • Show you what a demanding critique looks like, so you can recognize one
  • Be the subject itself: how to direct it, and how to judge what comes back

AI may not

  • Stand in for learning to write. "I used the tool" is not a skill you can show anyone

Whose work it is

AI may

  • Everything above, applied to work you produced
  • Work on your own material: your project, your data, your argument

AI may not

  • Produce work that you then submit as your own
  • Be used without being logged. An unlogged use is the thing that actually causes a problem

#### On the project sequence

The project sequence - the pitch, the three drafts, the poster and the final report. These sit on top of the W requirement and count toward none of it.

Where the first draft comes from does not matter here. This is the part of the course that is closest to professional practice, where the question is whether the work is right and whether you can stand behind it, not whose fingers typed the first version.

Project writing: what AI may and may not do

AI may

  • Generate the first draft of any project deliverable, in whole or in part
  • Rewrite, restructure or condense what you or your team has written
  • Draft the sections nobody wants to write, so the time goes to the analysis
  • Everything permitted on the writing assignments: finding sources, checking a claim, arguing against your position

AI may not

  • Put a claim in a deliverable that you cannot defend when a client asks
  • Submit analysis you did not check, on the grounds that the tool produced it
  • Use it without submitting the transcript. An unlogged use is the thing that is actually a problem
  • Paste client briefs or client data into a tool the university has not approved

A client does not care who drafted the sentence; they care whether the finding holds. The skill being graded in the project sequence is judgment about the work - and the AI Review Log, the rejected critiques and the transcript are how that judgment is evidenced, which is unchanged by relaxing the drafting rule.

If you are ever unsure which side of a line something falls on, the test is which assignment you are doing. On a W assignment the words you hand in are words you wrote. On project work, the test is whether you can defend the claim. In both cases everything you did is in the log and in the transcript.
