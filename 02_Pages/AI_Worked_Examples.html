AI Worked Examples

AI Toolkit · What good use actually looks like

 

Instructions only get you so far. Below are complete cycles — the prompt, what the AI actually said, what the student did with it, and the Review Log that resulted. Two of these deliberately show the AI being wrong, because that is the part you are graded on.

Example 1 — Hard Reviewer on an ethics essay draft

A strong cycle. The student accepts two critiques and rejects one, with a reason.

Student runs Hard Reviewer

Pastes the Hard Reviewer prompt with a 1,500-word draft arguing that location-data brokerage should require opt-in consent.

AI returns

  1. “Aggregated location data is effectively anonymous.” — This is contradicted by well-established re-identification research. You assert it in paragraph three and then argue against it in paragraph seven. Pick one.
  2. “The industry has failed to self-regulate.” — No evidence is offered. Which attempts, over what period, failing how? Without this the claim carries no weight.
  3. “Opt-in consent would eliminate the harm.” — Overclaimed. Consent regimes reduce harm; they do not eliminate it. Consent fatigue and dark patterns are documented failure modes you do not address.

Student's judgment

Accepted #1. It was right — I did contradict myself. Cut the paragraph-three sentence entirely.

Accepted #3. Softened to “substantially reduce” and added a paragraph on consent fatigue, which strengthened the argument.

Rejected #2. The claim is evidenced — the AI missed my citation to the FTC 2024 report two sentences later, which documents exactly the failed self-regulation it says is absent. I did move the citation adjacent to the claim so the link is unmissable, but the criticism itself was wrong.

Why this earns full points on the AI criterion

The student did not accept everything, and the rejection is specific and checkable — a named source the AI overlooked. They also made a small change in response to a wrong criticism, because a reviewer missing something twice is a signal about placement. That is exactly the judgment being assessed.

Example 2 — when the AI is confidently wrong

A GIS-specific failure. This one matters.

Student asks

“I am calculating built-up area change for Lagos from Landsat, 1990 to 2024. Here is my method. What am I missing?”

AI returns

A well-organized list of six considerations — atmospheric correction, cloud masking, classification accuracy, and so on. Among them:

“Compute area directly from the classified raster in WGS84 to preserve the original geometry.”

Student's judgment

Rejected, and it would have invalidated the entire analysis. WGS84 is a geographic CRS — degrees, not meters. Pixel area varies with latitude, so area computed that way is meaningless for change detection.

Reprojected to an appropriate equal-area projection for Nigeria before any area computation. Ran the Projection and Units Auditor persona afterward to check nothing else in the workflow had the same problem.

The lesson

The AI's other five points were good. This one was confidently stated, plausible-sounding, and would have produced a completely wrong answer with no error message.

This is why domain knowledge is not optional in an AI-assisted workflow. A student who could not spot this would have submitted invalid results with full confidence. You are the last line of defense, and you always will be.

Example 3 — the peer review protocol, end to end

Step 1 — own reading, no AI

Before opening any AI tool, the reviewer notes:

  • Strong: the data-fitness section is unusually honest about gaps
  • Strong: good figure showing the surveillance/detection overlap
  • Weak: I cannot tell what the actual finding is
  • Weak: the method section jumps straight to results with no validation

Step 2 — AI structured critique

AI returns five points. Three overlap with the reviewer's own reading. Two are new:

  • Inconsistent terminology — ‘detection zone’ and ‘surveillance area’ used interchangeably for what appear to be different things
  • The conclusion asserts a causal relationship the correlation shown does not support

It also claims the draft “lacks a literature review,” which the assignment did not ask for.

Step 3 — compare, and challenge

AI caught what I missed: the terminology inconsistency. I had read past it three times.

I caught what AI missed: the missing finding. AI critiqued the parts that existed and never noticed the whole document does not say what it concluded.

AI was wrong: the literature-review point. Not a requirement of this assignment. Ran Challenge the AI, which confirmed it was applying a generic academic-paper template rather than reading the brief.

Step 4 — the review, written

Leads with the missing finding, because it is the most important thing and the AI never found it. Includes the terminology point, credited to AI. Omits the literature review entirely. Ends with the two genuine strengths, named specifically.

What this demonstrates

The most valuable observation in that review — that the document never states its finding — came from a human reading it as a whole. The most easily missed detail came from the AI. Neither alone would have produced the review. That is the point of the protocol, and why the order is fixed.

Example 4 — a completed AI Review Log

What you attach to a submission. Short, specific, honest.

Sample completed AI Review Log
Tool(s) usedTAMU AI Chat (Claude Sonnet) for critique; M365 Copilot for the reference formatting pass.
Personas runHard Reviewer, Non-Technical Reviewer, Overclaim Detector. Also ran Prompt Critic first, which told me my original prompt had not specified the audience — that alone improved everything downstream.
Top critiquesHard Reviewer: self-contradiction on anonymity; unevidenced self-regulation claim; overclaimed consent remedy. Non-Technical: got lost at paragraph 5, did not know what action was being recommended. Overclaim Detector: four causal verbs where only correlation was shown.
Changes madeCut the contradictory sentence (¶3). Softened “eliminate” to “substantially reduce” and added a paragraph on consent fatigue. Rewrote ¶5 transition. Added an explicit recommendation in the closing. Replaced three causal verbs.
Critiques rejected(1) “Self-regulation claim unevidenced” — it is evidenced; the AI missed my FTC 2024 citation two sentences on. I moved the citation adjacent rather than adding one. (2) Overclaim Detector flagged “drives” in ¶9, but that sentence describes a mechanism established in the source I cite, not my own inference. Kept it. (3) Non-Technical Reviewer wanted the technical appendix simplified; the appendix is for the technical audience and simplifying it would defeat its purpose.

What makes this log strong

Three rejections, each with a different kind of reason: the AI missed evidence, the AI misread the epistemic status of a claim, and the AI gave advice that was right for one audience and wrong for another. A log with no rejections says either that you did not read critically, or that you did not push the AI hard enough to produce anything worth rejecting.
