How Projects Are Organized

GEOG 476 · GIS Practicum · Fall 2026

 

This course does not run on projects you invent. It runs on real problems brought to us by client organizations — and it is organized the way professional geospatial work is actually organized. Read this page carefully. It describes the structure you will work inside for the entire semester.

Two tiers: the team problem and your component

Every client brings a problem too large for one person and too interdependent for a group of strangers working separately. So the work splits in two:

The two tiers of project work
Tier 1 — The team projectA client supplies a real problem with real stakes. A team owns it end to end. The client is real, not a hypothetical.
Tier 2 — Your componentYou personally own one defined piece that the team’s answer depends on. Your component is your independent work, and it is what you are individually graded on.

Both tiers are real. A brilliant component that does not fit what your teammates built helps nobody. A well-coordinated team that produces shallow components helps nobody either.

The Project Manager role — read this before you volunteer

This is not the easy option

Every year, in every course that offers a role like this, someone takes it because it looks like less work than doing analysis. That is a serious misreading, and it is worth being blunt about it.

The Project Manager carries the consequences of everyone else’s problems. If a teammate owning a large component stops showing up in week nine, that is not primarily their crisis — it is yours. You are the person who has to have seen it coming, have a plan, and quite possibly do the work. If you want a lighter semester, own a component instead.

What the Project Manager actually does

  1. Scopes how the pieces fit. Maintains the interface diagram — what each component consumes and delivers — and catches gaps and overlaps before they become real.
  2. Runs the weekly intake. Every team member reports progress and blockers to you in Thursday studio. You are responsible for knowing the true state of the project, not the optimistic one.
  3. Maintains the risk register. A living document: what could go wrong, how likely, how bad, what the warning sign is, and what you will do about it.
  4. Writes contingency plans. For every component, an answer to: what happens if this fails, arrives late, or its owner disappears?
  5. Owns integration. When components are combined, you are accountable for whether they actually combine.
  6. Reports to the client. You are the voice of the project outside the team.

This writing is on top of your W requirement

To be clear about what this is not: your W-course writing requirement is already satisfied by the individual assignments — the Ethics Essay, Certification Essay, Resume and Portfolio. Project writing, management documents included, sits on top of that minimum. Status memos, risk registers and a post-mortem are exactly the writing project leads do in industry, and they are assessed to a professional standard — but they do not replace anything.

The Project Manager also owns a component

Management is not a way out of doing technical work. The Project Manager owns a small technical component alongside the management role — smaller than a full component owner’s, but real. You will be building something, not only tracking what others build.

How the Project Manager is graded

Basis of the Project Manager's grade
Management artifactsThe risk register, status memos, integration readiness reports and post-mortem — assessed on quality, honesty and timeliness. This is the largest share.
Your technical componentAssessed exactly as any component owner’s work is.
Project outcomePartly, but never wholly. You are accountable for coordination, not for a teammate’s disappearance. A project that hits trouble you identified early, escalated honestly, and planned around reflects well on you.

Why the role is worth taking anyway

Because it is the skill that gets people promoted. Most graduates can run an analysis. Far fewer can look at six moving parts, say honestly which one is going to fail, and have already planned for it. If you want to lead geospatial work rather than only perform it, this is the semester to find out whether you can.

Interface contracts

In week two, before any analysis begins, every component owner writes down two things:

  • What my component consumes — from whom, in what format, by when.
  • What my component delivers — to whom, in what format, by when.

The failure this prevents

The characteristic way component projects fail is not weak components. It is two people who each assumed the other was producing the shared layer — so nobody did, and nobody discovered it until November. An interface contract in week two costs one session and prevents exactly this.

When someone drops or falls behind

People withdraw from courses. People get ill. People underestimate a component. None of this is unusual, and none of it is treated as a catastrophe — it is treated as a project risk that should already have been identified.

  1. The risk register should already name the component as a dependency and state a warning sign.
  2. The Project Manager raises it in the weekly status memo as soon as the warning sign appears, not once the deadline is missed.
  3. The contingency plan is enacted — rescope, redistribute, or the manager absorbs the work.
  4. The instructor is informed through the status memo. Do not wait to be asked.

How this affects your grade

You are graded on your own component and your own contribution, not on whether a teammate disappeared. A team that identifies a problem early and manages it well is demonstrating exactly the skill this course exists to teach — and is assessed accordingly. A team that hides a problem until December is not.
