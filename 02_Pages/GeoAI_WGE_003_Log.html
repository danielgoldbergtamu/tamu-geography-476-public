1. The task, in the instructor's words

For the Africa project, I would like to generate some side by side imagery slides that demonstrate growth in a region. I would like the images to be satellite or aerial, and show before/after. I do not have a region in mind, and am fine with whatever city/region has two sets of before/after development available. There are probably analysis sources of similar things already online that might point us in the right direction, or you may just know. Does this make sense?

Note what he did NOT specify, because it matters to what happens next: no city, no dates, no sensor, no count of pairs beyond "two sets", and no statement of what the growth is supposed to demonstrate. He named a shape of evidence and left the evidence itself to be found. That is close to how a real client brief arrives.

2. What I understood it to be

Project 02 — Urbanization Rates in African Cities, client NGA Analysis Directorate, brief ATP-C-2026-0001, content/projects/urbanization.yaml. Paired images of the same place at two dates, side by side, so growth is visible without interpreting a chart.

Read from the project spine, unprompted, before searching:

  • The project's hard idea is stated in its own file: *"where does the city end" has no natural answer — administrative, built-up, commuting and lit boundaries give four different cities."*
  • Its components separate spread from sprawl, and separate infill, edge expansion and leapfrog.
  • Its imagery component's warning, verbatim from the file: *"Sensor changes, resolution changes and cloud cover all masquerade as urban growth."*
  • Its city list is an open unknown: *"The city list. NGA supplies it and it is not yet in hand, which blocks per-city component assignment."*

That last one decides something the instructor did not have to: whatever cities these slides use are teaching examples, not assignments, and the slide has to say so or students will build on a city list that does not exist yet.

I flagged "two sets" as ambiguous rather than guessing: two cities with one pair each, or one city at two different intervals, are different slides.

3. First move: find out what can actually be reached

This is the second time in two worked examples that the finding was not about geography at all: the analysis machine cannot reach the archive. Worth saying plainly to students — "I can read the page describing the image and I cannot download the image" is a strange and very modern kind of failure, and it is not fixed by knowing more remote sensing.

Note also what DID work: the page-reading tool retrieved the NASA pages, their sensors, their dates and their credit lines. Metadata came through; pixels did not.

6. Fetching, and being wrong about where the files went

Four images fetched inside his browser, on the page's own origin, and handed to the browser's download machinery. The fetches reported:

nairobi_tm5_1986005_lrg.jpg        status=200  bytes=2883645  image/jpeg
nairobi_oli_2016024_lrg.jpg        status=200  bytes=5339089  image/jpeg
beninurbaniz_etm_20020128_lrg.jpg  status=200  bytes=3273199  image/jpeg
beninurbaniz_oli2_20250111_lrg.jpg status=200  bytes=4401761  image/jpeg

Then nothing was in the Downloads folder. Nothing was downloading. No dialog was open — a screenshot confirmed the browser was sitting on the image, 2000 x 2000, perfectly happy.

I had asked him to approve access to a folder the files were never going to. His browser saves into the repository's own drop zone. He said so himself:

I have saved several files to the inbox.

All four were there, byte-for-byte the sizes the browser had reported. The lesson is not about browsers. It is that I confirmed the fetch and assumed the destination, and the two are different facts. The person watching knew the answer instantly because it was his machine.

The files were moved out of the drop zone into 03_Templates/images/ under their NASA names, and registered in config/images.yaml with the credit line, the source URL, the byte count and the pixel dimensions. The names were kept: nairobi_tm5_1986005 says Landsat 5 TM, 1986, day 005. nairobi_before.jpg would have said nothing.

7. The thing the pixel dimensions gave away

beninurbaniz_etm_20020128_lrg.jpg   (3064, 2979)
beninurbaniz_oli2_20250111_lrg.jpg  (3064, 2979)
nairobi_oli_2016024_lrg.jpg         (4000, 4000)
nairobi_tm5_1986005_lrg.jpg         (2000, 2000)

The Nairobi pair is published at two different resolutions over the same ground. Placed side by side as published, the 2016 frame is sharper — and a sharper picture of a city reads as more city. The project's own imagery component warns about this in the file I had read twenty minutes earlier: "sensor changes, resolution changes and cloud cover all masquerade as urban growth."

So the generator resamples both frames to the coarser grid before drawing them, and the figure says on its face that it did. Benin City needed nothing: identical dimensions, which is why it is the clean pair and Nairobi is the cautionary one.

Looking at the two Nairobi frames also showed a second confound with no fix: 1986 is dry and hazy, 2016 is green after rain. Both January, not the same January. Colour differences between them are not all change on the ground, and the figure says that too.

8. Building it

content/projects/urbanization_imagery.yaml first — the pairs, the dates, the sensors, what to look at, the caveats, and what is missing — so that none of the course prose lives in the Python. Then tools/slides/figs_urbanization.py to draw it.

Three things went wrong, none of which raised an error:

  1. The tagger refused the figure. The PDF tagger written earlier the same morning stopped the build: *"has no alt text in config/figures.yaml. A figure nobody described cannot be tagged; write the description first."* Correct refusal. The descriptions were written and it proceeded.
  2. The first render put the subtitle through the date labels, overflowed the caveat box, and printed the NASA credit on top of the last line of the footnote. Fixed positions in a footer whose content varies in length. Replaced with a running cursor. Found by looking at the PNG.
  3. The first deck version repeated the figure's title as the slide title, and the picture lost two inches of height to say the same thing twice. The figure slide now carries the kicker only. Found by reading the rendered PDF page.

The project deck generator had no way to place a picture at all — bullets, tables, quotes and statements only. A figure() slide was added to it, which pulls alternative text from config/figures.yaml and writes it into the .pptx shape, so the description travels with the picture.

Both slides are labelled TEACHING EXAMPLE, NOT AN ASSIGNMENT, because the NGA city list is not in hand and a student would otherwise start delineating Nairobi.
