Turn 2 — checking the map that had just been made

The question was asked in the abstract. It was answered by opening the map built two hours earlier and testing it.

The map: four small multiples of Texas counties, one per date, showing two thematic classes.

  • Gold — a county under a movement restriction.
  • Maroon — a county with a confirmed case somebody had published by name.

The defect: the two classes differed by HUE AND BY NOTHING ELSE.

That is a WCAG 1.4.1 failure — Use of Color — and on course material for a public university it is an ADA Title II exposure. But the accessibility citation is the least interesting part of it. It is a cartographic error first.

Why hue-only is a mapping mistake, not just a compliance one

Hue is one visual variable and it is a weak one for categorical separation. Bertin's set — position, size, value (lightness), texture, orientation, shape, hue — gives seven channels for encoding a class. A thematic map that spends only hue has spent the channel with the most failure modes:

Why hue-only is a mapping mistake, not just a compliance one
Failure modeWhat happens to a hue-only map
Color vision deficiencyRed-green CVD affects roughly 1 man in 12. Gold and maroon are two similar dark tones to that reader.
Grayscale printingBoth classes collapse to nearly the same gray.
Projector color balanceWarm-shifted projectors pull gold toward brown, and brown toward maroon.
Small areasA four-panel figure draws some counties a few millimeters across, where hue is hardest to judge.

And the failure is total rather than graceful. This map's entire argument is the difference between the two classes — restricted counties against counties with a named case, and the growing gap between them. A reader who cannot separate the classes does not get a degraded version of the argument. They get no argument at all, from a figure that looks finished.

How to test a map instead of assuming it

Three checks. None needs software the student does not have.

  1. Convert it to grayscale and look at it. If the classes are no longer distinguishable, the encoding was hue-only. This is the fastest honest test there is, and it takes ten seconds.
  2. Simulate color vision deficiency. Deuteranopia and protanopia are the two to check. Any image editor, several free web tools, and the simulation modes built into ArcGIS Pro and QGIS will do it.
  3. Look at it at the size it will actually be seen. On a projector from the back of the room, and at print size. A pattern that reads on a laptop can vanish at either extreme.

Do all three before the map is in a deliverable, not after. A map is usually made once and reproduced many times, in formats the author never sees.

The fix, and the principle under it

Redundant encoding. Every class carries at least two channels, so losing one still leaves the map readable.

The fix, and the principle under it
ClassBeforeAfter
Under movement restrictiongold fillgold fill plus a diagonal hatch
Confirmed case, county namedmaroon fillmaroon fill plus a heavy dark outline

Now the classes differ in hue, texture and outline weight. Convert it to grayscale: the hatched counties and the solid counties are still obviously different things.

Two more changes that matter as much and are easier to forget.

The legend moved onto the figure. Before, the key was a caption line underneath. A caption is not a legend. A figure travels — into a slide, a PDF, a web page, somebody's email — and the caption very often does not travel with it. If the key is not inside the image, the image is not self-describing.

Non-text contrast. WCAG 1.4.11 asks for 3:1 on graphical objects that carry meaning. People apply this to text and forget that map symbology is exactly what it was written for: a fill or a boundary line the reader has to distinguish from its surroundings is a meaningful graphical object.

The trap worth more than the rest of this log

The map had 263 words of alternative text and was still unreadable.

The alt text was good. It said what the figure established rather than what it depicted, it named the projection, it stated the precision of the coordinates. None of that helped the person this defect harms.

Because the reader harmed by a hue-only map can see the image perfectly and is not using a screen reader. Alt text serves someone who cannot see the figure. Color-blind readers are looking straight at it.

Alt text and color-safe symbology are two different accommodations for two different readers, and doing one well is not evidence of the other. That mistake is easy to make, it feels like diligence, and it is the reason this went out the door.

The workflow observation, stated professionally

The accessibility review in most shops looks at the document, not at the map inside it. A report gets checked for tagged headings, reading order, alt text and contrast on its text, and the figures are treated as images that need a description. A map is not an image that needs a description. It is an argument made in visual variables, and its accessibility is a property of the symbology.

If your quality process only asks whether a figure has alt text, it will pass every hue-only map you ever make.

The checklist, for your own project maps

You will each produce maps for a real client this semester. Run this on every one before it goes in a deliverable.

  • Two channels per class, minimum. Hue plus texture, shape, outline weight or value. Never hue alone.
  • Grayscale test. Convert and look. Ten seconds.
  • CVD simulation. Deuteranopia at least.
  • Legend on the figure, not in the caption.
  • 3:1 non-text contrast on any symbol carrying meaning.
  • Alt text that says what the map ESTABLISHES, not what it shows — and knowing it does not substitute for anything above.
  • Check it at delivery size, on a projector and in print.
  • State the projection, the data vintage and the source on the figure. Accessibility is about a reader being able to use your map; so is this.

One sentence to keep: a map that fails any of these is not a map with an accessibility problem, it is a map some of your readers cannot read, and those are the same sentence said honestly and dishonestly.
