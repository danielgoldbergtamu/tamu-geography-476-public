The difference between a useful answer and a plausible one is almost never the model. It is what you told it, and what you refused to accept back. These are the habits this course expects, and they are the ones an employer will notice.

The two that matter most

1. Tell it to ask you questions instead of assuming

A model will always produce an answer, because producing one is what it does. If you left something out, it fills the gap with an assumption you never saw it make — and then everything after that is built on the assumption.

"Before you answer, ask me anything you need to know. Do not guess."

Put it in your first message. Better still, standing instruction:

"If information is missing, ask me for it rather than assuming. Say explicitly when you are inferring something rather than working from what I gave you."

2. When you are working an idea out, make it interview you

This is the one nobody does, and it is the highest-value move in the whole list.

"I need to [thing]. Do not give me a plan yet. Ask me the five questions whose answers would most change what you would recommend, one at a time."

You are not using it to think for you. You are using it to find the part of your own thinking you have not done. It works because it has read a great many versions of your problem and knows where they usually go wrong.

Before you send

Say who it is for and what "good" looks like. Most disappointing output is an unstated standard. "Write a summary" gets you a generic one; "write a summary a county floodplain administrator could act on, one page, no jargon they would have to look up" gets you something usable.

Give it the constraints, not just the goal. What data you actually have, what you cannot get, the deadline, the software, what is off the table. A plan that assumes data you do not have is worse than no plan, because it looks like progress.

Say what you already tried and why it failed. Otherwise it will suggest it first, confidently, and you will have lost a round.

Show it one example of what good looks like if you have one. A single example beats three paragraphs describing the example.

Ask for options with trade-offs, not a recommendation — while you are still deciding. A recommendation hides the reasoning you need to see.

After it answers

Ask for the reasoning, then check the reasoning rather than the answer. The answer is easy to make plausible. The path to it is where the mistake is.

Ask what would have to be true for it to be wrong.

"What are you assuming here? What would change your answer?"

Make it argue against itself.

"Now give me the strongest case that this is the wrong approach."

If the counter-argument is weak, one of two things is happening: the idea is genuinely solid, or it is agreeing with you because you sounded confident. Push once more before you decide which.

Ask it to mark what it is unsure about, and to separate what it is citing from what it is inferring.

Never accept a number, a citation, a statute or a standard without checking it. These are exactly the things it produces most fluently and gets wrong most often. In this course, "the tool said so" is not a source, and a fabricated citation in a submitted assignment is an academic integrity matter regardless of where it came from.

Running a conversation

Correct it and continue, rather than starting over. "That is wrong, and here is why" is a normal move and most people never make it. It usually recovers in one turn.

But know when to start a clean chat. Once a conversation contains a bad assumption you have argued with three times, it is cheaper to open a new one and state the corrected version up front. Wrong context does not get diluted; it gets carried.

Do not ask for the whole thing at once. Break it up, check each piece, and keep the pieces you verified. A 2,000-word answer is 2,000 words you now have to audit.

Say when it is going the wrong way early. The cost of redirecting grows with every turn you let pass.

What unedited output looks like from the outside

Work that was prompted once and handed in unchanged is recognizable, and it is recognized. The tells are consistent enough to list:

  • Em dashes everywhere, several to a paragraph, used where a comma or a full stop would do.
  • A short sentence for emphasis. Dropped in after a long one, over and over, until the rhythm itself becomes the signature.
  • Every paragraph built the same way — topic sentence, three supporting sentences, conclusion — exactly as it was taught in a middle-school English class, and never varied.
  • Sentences that are all the same length, and all of them technically perfect. Real writing is lumpier than that.
  • British spellings appearing in an American document: colour, analyse, organisation. Some tools default to them and nobody notices.

None of these is proof of anything on its own, and none of them is banned. The reason to know them is that they are what your own writing starts to look like when you stop editing — and by the time a reader is counting em dashes, they have stopped reading for the argument.

Two things specific to this course

It is confidently wrong about GIS, specifically. Projections, what a statistic supports, join predicates, the conventions of a discipline it has only read about. See the AI Worked Examples page for a real one. This is not a reason to avoid it; it is the reason your verification is the thing being graded.

Write down what you rejected. Every graded assignment here carries an AI Review Log, and its highest-weighted field is the one recording the critiques you did not act on and why the reviewer was wrong. Accepting output is easy. Disagreeing with it in writing, and being right, is the skill.
