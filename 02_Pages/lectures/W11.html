What this session covers

  • How a technical talk differs from a general one — and how it does not. Technical audiences are not more patient, they are less.
  • How much method to include. Enough that a peer could evaluate your result, not so much that the finding disappears inside it.
  • Presenting uncertainty and negative results without apologizing for them.
  • Explaining a method to a mixed audience where some people know more than you and some know nothing about GIS.
  • Defending a technical choice — projection, classification, threshold, parameter — when someone asks why.
  • The demonstration that fails, and how to carry on.

How the session runs

Cut your own method down to what a peer needs to judge it

Twelve minutes, on your own method section.

The standard is not how much method is interesting. It is: enough that a peer could evaluate your result, and no more. Too little and they cannot tell whether to believe you. Too much and the finding disappears into the procedure — which is the failure mode technical people actually have, because the method is the part we are proud of.

The test, and it is the same one the room applies afterward: from what I just said, could a peer in this field decide whether my result is trustworthy?

Two get read out, and the room answers exactly that question — not whether it was clear, and not whether it was interesting. Could you evaluate the result from that? If the answer is no, the room names the one thing that was missing, which is usually a number.

Which questions were hardest, and which you should have seen coming

Eight minutes, after the paired defense of a technical choice.

Each person names the hardest question they were asked, in the words it was asked in. Then the room sorts them into two piles, and the sorting is the exercise.

  • Questions you could not have anticipated. Genuinely new information, or an angle from somebody else's expertise. These are rare, and they are the good ones — write them down, because they usually improve the work.
  • Questions you should have seen coming. Somebody asked about the choice you were least comfortable with. That is not bad luck. The question you dread is the question a competent audience asks, because your discomfort is a signal about the weak point and other people can hear it in how you describe it.

The second pile is nearly always bigger, and it is the useful one: it is the list of things to have an answer for before the dry run next week, and before a stranger asks in the hall the week after.

An answer you have prepared does not have to be a defense. "I chose 500 meters because that is the standard in this literature, I tested 250 and 1,000, and the pattern holds at all three" is a prepared answer. So is "I do not know whether that changes the result, and it is the first thing I would test." Both are stronger than being surprised.
