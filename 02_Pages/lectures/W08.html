What this session covers

  • What a portfolio is for, and the different kinds: a personal site, a GitHub profile, an Esri Story Map, a PDF, a LinkedIn presence. Each reaches a different reader.
  • What belongs in one, and what does not. Three strong pieces beat nine mediocre ones, and one weak piece drags down everything next to it.
  • How to present a piece: the problem, what you did, what you found, and what you would do differently. That last one is what makes it read as a professional rather than a student.
  • Showing work that belongs to a team — how to be honest and specific about your own contribution.
  • Critiquing a portfolio, including your own.
  • What can and cannot go public from a client project, and why that question has to be asked before you post anything.

Choose your three, and write down what each one shows and your hardest cut

Ten minutes. Pick the three pieces of work you would put in a portfolio, then write down what each one shows and which cut was hardest.

Choosing three is the exercise. A portfolio of nine is not more evidence — one weak piece drags down everything next to it, because a reader who is deciding whether to trust you reads the weakest thing as the honest sample.

Two rules while you choose, and they are the ones people break.

  • Select on what a piece demonstrates, not on what it cost you. The project that took a hundred hours and shows one skill loses to the one that took twelve and shows judgment. Sunk effort is invisible to a reader and enormous to you, which is why you will defend the cut out loud.
  • Each of the three should earn its place for a different reason. Technical depth, communication, and honest handling of a result that did not work is a stronger set than three analyses. Three of the same thing tells a reader one fact about you.

Then be ready to defend the cut. When I call on you, I will ask what the piece you kept shows that the piece you dropped does not.

What people cut, and which cut was hardest

Six minutes. I call on people around the room for one thing they cut and why, in a sentence.

The room is listening for a pattern, and it is reliably this one: the hardest cut is nearly always the piece with the most work in it. When that comes up, name it out loud, because recognizing it is what makes the same decision possible in eighteen months when the stakes are a job rather than an exercise.

Two questions to close on.

  • Did anybody cut something because it did not work? If so, consider putting it back. A piece where you explain what failed, what you learned and what you would do differently is often the strongest thing in a portfolio, because almost nobody includes one — and it is the only kind of piece that demonstrates judgment rather than capability.
  • Whose selection tells a story about a person, rather than listing work? That is the difference between a portfolio and a folder.
