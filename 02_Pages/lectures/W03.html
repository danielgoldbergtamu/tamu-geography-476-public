What this session covers

  • Your ranked three, handed in — and how teams and Project Managers get built out of them.
  • What a project management plan actually contains — and why every organization uses a slightly different set of components. Three elements, four, five, ten: the number matters less than whether the plan survives contact with reality.
  • Budgeting a project: people, time, data, software, and the costs students routinely forget.
  • Interface contracts — the promise you make to your teammates about what you will hand them, in what format, by when.
  • Spatial data standards, national and international, and why they exist. Format, coordinate reference system, metadata standards, published specifications. A dataset without documented standards is a dataset you cannot defend.
  • What the Project Manager owns, and what they do not. It is not the easy option and it is not the boss.
  • Doing project management with AI, and the rule that governs it. Where AI actually helps in running a project — drafting scope, surfacing dependencies you left unstated, prompting for budget lines you forgot, generating candidate risks, compressing a status update — and where it is confidently, fluently wrong: scope that would fit any project, invented durations, prices it cannot know, and a risk register that is nobody's in particular. Worked live against one of the two client briefs: ask it where the schedule breaks if a component slips two weeks; ask it what dependencies between components are unstated; ask it for the top five risks and then ask the room whether those are our risks or anyone's. The rule: you may not adopt a method you cannot defend independently of the tool that suggested it — and a plan is a method. This is what the AI Method Log records, and it is required on every project deliverable from Draft 1 onward.
  • Doing GIS with AI is Thursday, not today. The workflow-level version — data discovery, coordinate systems, tool and parameter choice, scripting, debugging, quality assurance, cartographic critique, and the ArcGIS Pro demonstration that goes with it — moved to S02 so that this session can finish the collection and the budgeting properly.

How the session runs

What you hand in today

Everything from this session goes into Attendance 1, in this module — a Canvas text entry, written on your own laptop in the room. Two things, both one sentence each:

  1. Your ranked three components, in order, with one sentence after each on why you — plus Project Manager: yes, no, or would consider. This is what teams are built from, and there is no second collection.
  2. The line you would refuse to put your name on, from the verify-or-reject drill, and how you would have caught it.

The AI-written plan for that drill is its own page in this module — Verify or reject: an AI-written project plan. One screen between two of you.

Nothing in this course is printed. Everything you need is in Canvas on your own machine.

Before you arrive

  • Bring your S01 decomposition. If you have not written your ranked three yet, that is the first thing you will do in this room — come with the decomposition and you have what you need.
  • Think about what you would need from a teammate in order to start your own piece.

What this sets up

S02 on Thursday is where you claim your component formally and write its interface contract. Project Draft 1 is your first substantial written deliverable, and the data standards discussed today are part of what it is assessed on.
