Which poster would you walk over to?

I will show three real GIS posters, one at a time and full screen. We do this as a class: look at them from your seat, and I will call on you one by one. Tell us which poster you would walk over to, what you could read on each one, and what on Posters 1, 2 and 3 was too small to read. Be specific: "the left column is too small to read" is useful, and "too much text" is not.

After each poster comes a heatmap of the same poster. Red and yellow mark where a viewer's eyes are predicted to land first and linger; blue marks light attention. A box with an arrow says why the eye goes to each hot spot, such as a large title on a dark band, the only strong color on the page, or a photo of people. The heatmaps come from a computer model of visual attention, not from eye-tracking anyone, so treat them as a prediction to argue with. The model gives the maps and figures extra weight, because a geography audience goes to the maps first.

What could you read from your seat?

I will call on you one by one: did the heatmap match where your eyes went, which map pulled your eye first, and what the poster made you hunt for. From across a room, people can read a short title, one large map and strong colors. Little else.

Then check your team's poster for three common problems:

  • The title is too long. A title that states your finding works better than one that names your topic.
  • Two maps the same size compete. Pick one and make it the largest thing on the poster.
  • There is too much text. Your text explains what your map should show.

On a team poster, a reader should also be able to tell which part is yours.

Cut a paragraph to a third

Use about 500 words on the whole poster, at most. Three ways to shorten a paragraph:

  1. Delete sentences that say what you are about to say. Nobody reads a poster from the top.
  2. Delete filler words such as relatively, fairly, in general and it should be noted.
  3. Delete sentences that describe the map. If the map does not show it, fix the map.

We do this as a class. Look at the paragraph below, and I will call on you one by one: which sentences only set up what comes next, which filler words can go, which sentence the map makes unnecessary, and which one has to stay. Keep the finding and the two numbers.

The paragraph to cut

The paragraph is also on the screen while you work.

In this project, we set out to examine how built-up area has changed over time in six cities. It should be noted that the results presented here are preliminary in nature. As can be seen in the map to the left, the built-up area of City B has grown considerably between 2005 and 2025, roughly doubling in size over the twenty-year study period. In general, road length grew relatively little over the same period, at about 9 percent. This suggests that, at least in City B, new building has spread outward to areas that the existing road network does not reach.

One cut version: City B's built-up area doubled from 2005 to 2025, while its roads grew 9 percent. New building spread where the roads do not reach.

Three kinds of visitor

Like our AI reviewer personas, different visitors read the same poster with different questions:

Three kinds of visitor
VisitorWhat they look at
Newcomer, who knows no geographyThe title, photos, drawings and bright color. They skip legends and methods.
GIS basicsThe maps first, then the legend, scale and title. They ask what the map shows.
ExpertThe maps, then the legend, method and results text. They ask how the map was made and whether to trust it.

Two posters from last year, Food Deserts and Heat Wave Vulnerability, are shown first as they are, then three times each, with the original beside a heatmap of where each visitor is predicted to look and boxes saying why. These come from a computer model of attention with different weights for each visitor, not from eye-tracking.
