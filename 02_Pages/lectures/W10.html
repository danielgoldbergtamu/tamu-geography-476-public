What this session covers

  • A poster is not a paper on a wall. It is a conversation starter that has to work while you are talking to somebody else.
  • Designing for three reading distances: three seconds from across the hall, thirty seconds from a few feet, three minutes with you standing next to it.
  • Layout, visual hierarchy, and the discipline of white space.
  • Cutting text ruthlessly. Roughly 500 words on the whole poster. Every word is competing with your figures.
  • Making the map or figure the centerpiece rather than an illustration.
  • On a team poster: making sure a reader can tell what you contributed.
  • Printing — size, resolution, bleed, cost, and how long it actually takes.

How the session runs

Three real posters at 25% — write down what survives and what disappears

Twelve minutes. Three real conference posters go up on the screen at 25% of their printed size — roughly what a poster looks like from across a hall, which is the distance at which somebody decides whether to walk over.

You do not read them. You write down two lists.

  • What survives. What can you still make out — the title, one figure, a block of color, a number?
  • What disappears. What is definitely there and is doing nothing at that size?

Be concrete. "The text disappears" is not a finding; "the whole left column is gray" is.

The three reading distances, which is what the exercise is teaching you to design against:

What survived, and what that predicts about your own

Seven minutes. The room reads out what survived on each of the three, and the lists agree more than people expect: a short title, one large figure, and strong contrast. Almost nothing else does.

Then the question that makes it useful: what does that predict about the poster you are currently designing?

Three predictions that are true nearly every time.

  • Your title is too long. If it did not survive at 25% on somebody else's poster, yours will not either. A title that states the finding beats one that states the topic.
  • You have more than one candidate centerpiece. Two figures competing at three seconds means neither wins, and the reader keeps walking.
  • Your text is doing work your figure should do. Which is the next exercise.

Cut a paragraph of your own to a third of its length

Eleven minutes, on a paragraph from your own poster draft.

A whole poster is roughly 500 words. That is not a target, it is a ceiling that most posters exceed by a factor of three — and every word competes with your figures for the same thirty seconds.

Cut one paragraph to a third. Not summarize — cut. Three passes:

  1. Delete every sentence that says what you are about to say. Setup sentences are for documents that get read from the top, and nobody reads a poster from the top.
  2. Delete every qualifier that does not change what a reader would do. Relatively, fairly, it should be noted that, in general.
  3. Replace description of a figure with the figure. If a sentence tells the reader what the map shows, the map is not showing it, and the sentence is the wrong fix.

What is left is usually better rather than merely shorter, and that is the finding to keep: on a poster, cutting is a design tool rather than a compromise.

Questions about the day itself

Four minutes at the end, held deliberately, because the logistics are the part that goes wrong and the print deadline is hard.

Bring the questions you actually have. The ones that come up every year:

  • The submission is a hard 9:00 AM deadline, and it is a print deadline rather than an upload deadline — the file has to be right when it is sent, because there is no revision after it goes to print.
  • What size and format the boards take, and what happens if your file is the wrong ratio.
  • What you stand with, and for how long — you are with your poster for the session, talking to whoever comes.
  • The one-minute presentations are timed and in order, and the order is assigned from what was submitted.
  • Who is coming. Visitors from outside the university arrive part way through, which is when the room changes from classmates to strangers.
