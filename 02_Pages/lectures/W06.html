What this session covers

  • What makes an ethical GIS professional, according to the people who had to write it down: the GISCI Code of Ethics and the URISA GIS Code of Ethics.
  • The four obligation sets those codes are built on — to society, to employers and funders, to colleagues and the profession, and to individuals in society — and what happens when two of them conflict, which is the only interesting case.
  • Illegal, against professional standards, and merely unwise are three different categories. Telling them apart is most of the work.
  • Where geospatial work is ethically distinctive: locational privacy, aggregation, inference about people who are not your subject, dual use, and the authority a map carries whether or not it has earned it.
  • Working through real cases, including ones drawn from this semester's client projects.
  • Two AI policies written for two GEOINT courses, pointing in opposite directions — including the one you are working under, which is not the only defensible answer.

How the session runs

What today is not: this is not a compliance briefing

A compliance briefing tells you the rules and asks you to acknowledge that you have been told. Nothing today works like that, and if you sit through it waiting to be told what is allowed you will leave with nothing usable.

The reason is that the codes do not resolve the cases you will actually meet. The GISCI and URISA codes are clear, agreed, and silent on exactly the situation that will land on you — because the situations that matter are the ones where two obligations you accept both point in opposite directions, and no code can rank them for you in advance.

So today is practice at deciding, out loud, with somebody disagreeing. What you are being graded on later is not whether you chose the way I would have; it is whether you can name the obligation you sacrificed and say why.

The case, in threes

Your employer wants a map published. You think it misleads — not that it is false, which would be easy, but that a reader will take away something the data does not support.

The specifics, which are what make it hard:

  • The map is technically accurate. Every number on it is correct, and you produced them.
  • The classification breaks were chosen by somebody else, and they flatter the result. Different breaks, equally defensible, would show a much weaker pattern.
  • Your employer has a real interest in the stronger version, and has not asked you to lie about anything.
  • The audience is the public, and will not see the classification choice at all.

In threes, fifteen minutes. Decide as a group and produce two sentences.

  1. What do you do? Publish, refuse, publish with a change, or escalate — and be specific about what the change or the escalation actually is.
  2. Which obligation are you sacrificing? Name it from the four: to society, to employers and funders, to colleagues and the profession, or to individuals in society. Every option on the list sacrifices one of them, and a group that claims otherwise has not finished the case.

Do not spend your fifteen minutes deciding whether the map is misleading. Assume it is. The exercise is the choice that follows.

Who chose what, and what it cost them

Each group reports two things and nothing else: what they chose, and which obligation they gave up. Nine minutes, and the second half is the part that matters — a group that reports only the choice is asked for the cost before the next group goes.

The pattern to watch for is that the same choice arrives with different costs attached, which tells you the groups were reasoning from different obligations rather than disagreeing about facts.

Where the room disagreed, and why disagreement is the point

Eight minutes, and the aim is not to converge.

A room that agrees on a case like this has usually flattened it — by assuming the employer is acting in bad faith, or by inventing an option that removes the conflict. When that happens the case stops teaching, because the choice that made it hard has been designed away.

Two questions:

  • Where did the room split, and what was the split actually about? Almost always it is which obligation is ranked highest, not what the facts were.
  • Which position would be hardest to defend in writing, to somebody who disagreed? That one is your ethics essay. A dilemma with an obvious answer is not a dilemma, and an essay about one has nothing to argue.

What the essay comes out of

The essay you write next is about a dilemma from your own project, not from a published case. Today is where you learn what a real one feels like from the inside, so that you recognize yours when it turns up in October.

The second case, and you are inside this one

The first case was somebody else's. This one is yours, and it is live.

You are required to use AI in this course. You are graded on how well you direct and judge it, never on whether you used it, and using it without logging it is the thing that is actually a problem here.

Another GEOINT course, at another university, hands its students a document called Draft AI policy for Introduction to Geospatial Intelligence (not included in the public copy — a third-party document, shared with this class under a permission that does not extend to redistribution). It is one page; read it before you argue about it. It sets the opposite default: students do not use AI on any assignment except where that assignment specifically permits it, and unauthorized use is reviewed against the university's academic misconduct policy.

That course is not confused, and neither is this one. Read what its policy is protecting:

to develop the human intelligence skills that AI cannot do well — reflecting, ambiguity tolerance, associative thinking, abductive thinking, imaginative thinking, negative awareness, and ethical thinking

That is a sharper list of what these tools cannot do than anything on our syllabus. And the reasoning underneath it is the same as ours: no intelligence agency is using AI as a substitute for human accountability for thought or action, so a graduate needs to bring more than AI skills to the work.

Same destination. Opposite route. Both defensible.

Six minutes, whole room. Two questions.

  • What is each policy protecting, and what is it willing to lose to protect it? Ours risks a graduate who never built the unassisted skill, because the tool was always there. Theirs risks a graduate who cannot direct a tool every employer assumes they can direct. Neither risk is imaginary.
  • Which one would be harder to defend to a student who lost points under it? Answer honestly. This is not a question with a house answer, and several of you will sit under both regimes — the work you hand off from this course goes to INTA 670.

You do not have to resolve this. You have to be able to state the other side's obligation without making it stupid, which is the whole skill this session is about, and it is much harder when the policy you are arguing against is one you benefit from.
