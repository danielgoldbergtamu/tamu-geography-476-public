What this session covers

  • The reader who does not have time. Everything today follows from one fact: your audience will stop reading before the end, and may only read the first paragraph.
  • Answer first. Nearly every profession has invented this format and given it a different name — the executive summary in business, the inverted pyramid in journalism, the abstract in research, SBAR in medicine, and BLUF — Bottom Line Up Front — in defense and intelligence work. They are the same discipline: state your conclusion, then support it.
  • How to write one. Finding first, confidence in it second, what it rests on third, what would change it last.
  • Stating confidence honestly. The difference between "we assess with high confidence", "the evidence is consistent with", and "we cannot determine" — and why using the strongest one you can get away with destroys your credibility permanently the first time you are wrong.
  • What you leave out. A decision-maker does not need your method unless it changes what they should do.
  • Where this format is wrong: it is bad for teaching, bad for narrative, and bad when the reasoning is the point.
  • Working from your own project: writing the answer-first opening for your own component.

How the session runs

Two openings on screen. Which one does a busy reader finish, and why

Eight minutes. Two openings to the same piece of work go on the screen. You read both and vote, and then you say why — the why is the whole activity, because the vote is never close.

Opening A. "This report examines urbanization in six African cities between 2005 and 2025. We begin by describing the study area and the available imagery, then set out the classification approach used to derive built-up extent, before presenting results and discussing their implications for infrastructure planning."

Opening B. "Three of the six cities we measured doubled their built-up area in twenty years. Two of them did it with no matching expansion of the road network, which is the finding with the clearest infrastructure consequence. The measurement is repeatable anywhere Landsat coverage exists. Details follow."

Both are honest, both are competently written, and only one of them is finished by a reader who stops after four sentences.

What actually made the difference

Six minutes, and the answers are more specific than "B is shorter" — because B is not much shorter.

Three differences do the work.

  • A tells you what the document will do. B tells you what it found. A promises; B delivers. A reader who stops after A has learned nothing except that a report exists.
  • B puts the decision-relevant clause where the reader is still reading. No matching expansion of the road network is the sentence somebody acts on, and in A it is on page nine.
  • A's structure is the order the work happened in. That order is a record of your effort. Your reader is not interested in your effort; they are deciding something, and the order that serves them is the reverse.

This discipline has five names across five professions — journalism calls the shape an inverted pyramid and the opening sentence a lede, the military and the intelligence community call it bottom line up front, consulting calls it answer first. They are the same instruction, arrived at independently by every profession whose readers are short of time. That convergence is the argument.

What follows it is not "then nothing": bottom line, then so what, then the supporting detail, and everything else in an annex. The annex is not a demotion — it is where you put the material that would cost you the reader.

What you had to throw away in order to lead with the answer

Eight minutes at the close, after you have rewritten your own project pitch opening and had somebody mark it.

One question: what did leading with the answer cost you? Everybody loses something, and naming it is what stops this becoming a formatting rule you apply without thinking.

The four things people report throwing away, and what each one usually means:

What you had to throw away in order to lead with the answer
What you threw awayWhat it usually means
The context you thought was needed firstSome of it was. One sentence of it goes before the finding; the rest goes after, and the test is whether the finding is unintelligible without it
The caveatsThey moved, they did not vanish. A caveat after the claim is read; a caveat before it makes the claim look unfinished
The narrative of how you got thereThis is the real loss, and it is worth about ten seconds of regret. It belongs in the method section, where a peer will read it
The strength of the claimIf you had to weaken the finding to state it in one sentence, the finding was overclaimed all along and the structure just showed you

That last row is the one to take seriously. Leading with the answer is uncomfortable mostly when you do not have one, and discovering that today is considerably better than discovering it in the executive summary.
