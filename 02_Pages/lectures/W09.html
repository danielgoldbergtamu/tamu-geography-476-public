What this session covers

  • What actually makes a presentation work, drawn from the room's own experience — the best and worst you have each sat through.
  • Structure: lead with the finding, not the method. A busy audience should not have to wait to learn why they are listening.
  • Designing slides people can follow while you talk, rather than read instead of listening to you.
  • Delivery: pace, pauses, what to do with your hands, and how to recover when something goes wrong — because it will.
  • Handling questions, including the ones you cannot answer. "I do not know — here is what would tell us" is a strong answer, not a weak one.
  • Timing, and why running over is a failure of preparation rather than of enthusiasm.

How the session runs

Rewrite your opening, finding first

Eight minutes, on your own, on your actual project. Write in your notes.

Most technical openings are built the way the work was done: background, then data, then method, then — eventually — what was found. That order is a record of your effort and it makes a listener wait to learn why they are listening. Reverse it. Say what you found, then how you know.

Rewrite your opening, finding first
The opening you haveThe opening you want
"Our project looks at urbanization in African cities. We used Landsat imagery from 2005 to 2025, classified built-up area, and compared the results across a set of cities.""Three of the six cities we measured doubled their built-up footprint in twenty years, and two of those did it without any matching growth in the road network. Here is how we measured it."

The test: if a listener walked out after your first two sentences, would they know what you found? If not, the finding is still buried, however good the sentences are.

Two failure modes to check your own line against.

  • The finding is a topic, not a result. "We looked at the relationship between X and Y" names a subject. "X rose while Y did not" is a finding.
  • The hedge arrives before the claim. "Although our data has limitations, and this is preliminary, it may suggest…" — the caveats are honest and they belong in the second half. Lead with the claim you are prepared to defend, then say what it rests on.

Bad and good: what a slide the room can follow looks like

A slide the room can follow while you talk makes one point, and its title says what the point is. Three pairs on the slides show the same content done badly and well:

  • A wall of text and one point with a chart. The bad slide holds every word of the result, so the room reads instead of listening. The good slide's title is the finding, and the chart shows it.
  • A map nobody can read and one that makes a point. The bad map is small, in nine rainbow colors, with a legend in tiny type. The good map fills the slide, uses two or three colors, and its title says what it shows.
  • A table of numbers and the one comparison that matters. The bad slide makes the room hunt through every number. The good slide shows the one comparison, with the answer highlighted.

The cities and numbers in the examples are invented for teaching.

Rules for your slides

Slides should add to what you are saying, not replace it. Never read them to the room. They cue you, and they are not a script.

  • One slide per minute. A ten-minute talk has about ten slides.
  • A picture on every slide. A map, a chart or a photo. The best slides have no words at all.
  • Contact information on the first and the last slide: your name and email.
  • Branding. Your team's and the client's names and logos, used the same way on every slide.
  • No animations. They distract the room and break on other computers.
  • Colors that are easy to look at. Dark text on a light background, or the reverse. Never yellow, which disappears on a projector.
  • Plan for the worst setup. Check that your slides read at low resolution, and keep every file, video and map on your laptop in case there is no internet.

If your talk includes a demo

  • Pre-record it, and play the recording instead of running the demo live.
  • Keep the recording on your laptop, not on a website or a shared drive.
  • Make no last-minute changes to the demo, the data or the slides on the day of the talk.

How to be less nervous

  • The room is not judging you. Most people are there because they were required to be, not to grade you.
  • Most of the room is half listening. A small slip goes unnoticed.
  • Get a laugh early. Start with a Howdy, or make fun of yourself.
  • Know your subject. The better you know it, the less a hard question can shake you.
  • Have the answers ready. Put slides for the questions you expect after your last slide.

Stand up and say your opening

Ten minutes. I will call on people around the room to stand up and say their opening, in thirty seconds, without notes.

Stand up. Saying your opening out loud exposes what reading it silently does not: where you run out of breath, where you hedge without meaning to, and whether you know your finding well enough to say it without looking.

After each speaker, I will ask the room two questions, in this order:

  1. What did they find? Someone says it back in their own words. If nobody can, the opening failed, however well it was delivered.
  2. When did you know? The first sentence, the second, or not yet. That is the score.

Then we will discuss what that speaker should move earlier. No advice on delivery yet. An opening that is well delivered, with the finding in the wrong place, still has the finding in the wrong place, and fixing the structure is worth more this week than fixing the pace.

Answering a question you did not expect

  • Listen to the end. Let the person finish, then say the question back in one sentence.
  • Answer, then stop. Give the answer first and the reasons after it.
  • A test question. Answer the point, not the tone. Do not argue.
  • Steer it. Answer the question you want to answer, not the one you were asked. Take the question in one sentence, then bring it to the point you came to make.
  • You do not know. Say "I do not know. Here is what would tell us." That is a strong answer.

Have answers ready after your last slide

A professional has the answer ready before the question is asked. Before your talk:

  1. Write the five questions you are most likely to be asked.
  2. Have a teammate ask the hardest question they can think of, and add it to the list.
  3. Build one backup slide for each question and put them after your closing slide, where the room does not see them.
  4. When the question comes, go straight to that slide and answer from it.

What did leading with the finding cost?

Five minutes. I will call on a few of you with one question: what did you have to give up to lead with the finding?

The answers repeat every year, and each one is worth naming.

  • "It felt like I was overclaiming." Usually the finding was stated more strongly than the evidence supports — which is a real problem, and the fix is a more precise finding rather than a delayed one.
  • "They will not understand it without the background." Sometimes true. Then the fix is one sentence of context before the finding, not four minutes.
  • "It felt arrogant." It is not. A busy listener experiences a delayed finding as a demand on their time, and the confident version is the one that respects it.
