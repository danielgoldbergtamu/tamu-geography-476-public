What this session covers

  • What a resume is actually for, and who reads it — a screening system first, a recruiter for six seconds second, a hiring manager third. Each wants something different.
  • The sections, and what belongs in each: contact, education, experience, skills, leadership.
  • Tailoring to a specific job advertisement, which is the whole assignment and the thing most people never do.
  • Evidence over assertion: scale, tools, outcome. Why "built a suitability model in ArcGIS Pro covering 254 Texas counties" beats "experience with GIS analysis."
  • How to critique a resume — including your own, which is harder.

How the session runs

Your resume has three readers, and only one of them is a person with time

The same document is read three times, by readers who want different things and who each spend a different amount of attention on it. Writing for one and forgetting the others is how a good resume fails.

Only the recruiter is a person with time, and six seconds is not much of it. The other two are a machine that cannot infer and a specialist who will not guess.

Reader 1 — the software, and why the keywords come from the advertisement

An applicant tracking system is a database, not a judge. It parses your resume into fields — name, employer, dates, titles, skills — stores them, and lets a recruiter search and filter. The widely repeated claim that a robot rejects three quarters of resumes on its own is worth treating with suspicion; the thing that actually happens is more ordinary and more fixable. A human being types words into a search box, and resumes that do not contain those words are not in the results. You were not rejected. You were not returned.

Which is why the keywords come from the advertisement and nowhere else. The person searching wrote that posting, or was handed it, and they will search using its language. If the posting says geospatial analysis, that phrase belongs on your page even if you have been writing GIS analysis for four years. If it names ArcGIS Pro, Python and FEMA flood data, those are the strings being searched for.

Four rules follow, and none of them require writing badly.

  • Use the posting's exact terms, in your own true sentences. Matching vocabulary is not the same as claiming experience you do not have.
  • Spell out the acronym and give the abbreviation once — coordinate reference system (CRS) — because you do not know which one was typed.
  • Keep the structure boring. Standard headings, one column, no tables, no text boxes, no text inside a logo. A parser that cannot find your Experience section has not found your experience.
  • Never keyword-stuff. A block of terms at the foot of the page is read by the machine and by the recruiter, and the second one draws the conclusion you would expect.

Evidence, not assertion — and the rewrite

An assertion says you have a skill. Evidence says what you did with it, at what scale, and what came of it. Three components: the method, the context, and the outcome.

Evidence, not assertion — and the rewrite
Assertion"Experience with GIS analysis." Unfalsifiable, and therefore worth nothing to a reader who is trying to distinguish you from forty other applicants
Method and context only"Built a suitability model in ArcGIS Pro covering 254 Texas counties." Much better. A reader can picture the work
All three"Built a suitability model in ArcGIS Pro across 254 Texas counties, reducing the candidate site list from 1,900 to 40 for field verification." The outcome is what makes it a contribution rather than an activity

Rewrite one of your own bullets with all three in it. Twelve minutes. Use your project component if your work history is thin — that is what it is there for, and by December it will carry a real client and a real result.

Three rewrites read out; the room judges whether each is checkable

Three volunteers read the bullet they just wrote. The room answers one question about each, and it is the same question this course asks of everything:

Could somebody check it?

Not is it impressive, and not does it read well. Checkable means a person could ask you a question about it and you would have an answer with a number in it — how many counties, over what period, and what happened to the 40?

Three things the room listens for.

  • A number that is doing no work. "Analyzed thousands of records" sounds like scale and cannot be checked, because it is not a claim about anything in particular.
  • An outcome that is really an activity. "...to support decision-making" is what you hoped it did. "...which the county used to prioritize inspections" is what happened.
  • A method you could not be questioned on. If the bullet names a tool you touched once, it will be the first thing asked about in the interview. Every line on this page is an invitation to a question, and you are choosing which questions you get.
