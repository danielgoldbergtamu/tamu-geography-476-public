Notice

The GIS Day debrief is part of your presentation

There is no separate debrief session. What you learned in that hall is a required part of what you present, graded inside the presentation rather than as an exercise nobody outside the room ever sees. Every team answers three things explicitly: what a stranger did not understand, what you changed because of it, and what you decided not to change and why.

Sort what you heard into three piles before you build this — fix it, explain it better, or disagree with it. Most feedback that feels like an attack is the second pile: the work was fine and the explanation was not. Where three people asked the same question, that is one problem with your framing, not three questions.

What this session covers

  • Answer first: the finding, then what it rests on.
  • Every member presents their own component and takes questions on it.
  • Information gaps are a headline, not an apology. Naming precisely what is unknown, and what would most reduce that uncertainty, is real value to the client.
  • The handoff: what you are leaving behind for the students who continue this.

How the session runs

Set up, order confirmed, timing rules restated

The first five minutes, before the first team starts. Short, and worth doing properly because the schedule has no slack in it.

  • Set up. Slides were submitted in advance; confirm yours is the file loaded, that it opens, and that every member who is presenting knows where they stand.
  • Order confirmed. Which teams present today and which present Thursday is announced here, and it does not change — if you are today, you are today.
  • Timing rules restated, because they are enforced rather than advisory.

Two things to have settled before you begin, both of which were decided in the dry run.

  • Who answers what. A question that arrives at the team and lands on nobody costs you thirty seconds in front of a client audience. Agree the map beforehand and hand off out loud: "that is my component — ".
  • What you say when you do not know. "We do not know that, and here is what would tell us" is a complete, strong answer. Bluffing to a client audience is the one failure that cannot be recovered from, because everything else you said becomes suspect at the same moment.

Teams presenting Thursday: watch today and take notes. What the cross-team questions probe is nearly identical from team to team, and you have two days to have those answers ready.
