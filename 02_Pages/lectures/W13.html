What this session covers

  • A public audience — other students, faculty, staff, visitors from outside the university, and people who know more about your topic than you do.

How the session runs

Arrive, set up, posters on boards, files confirmed

The first ten minutes of the event are yours, and they are the ten minutes that go wrong most often. Do them in this order.

  1. Arrive before your slot, not at it. The order is fixed and the schedule does not wait for somebody who is still finding the room.
  2. Get your poster on its board. Boards are assigned; find yours before you do anything else. Bring nothing to fix it with and assume nothing is provided — pins, clips or tape, whichever the boards take.
  3. Confirm your file is the one that will be shown. Everything was submitted at the hard 9:00 AM deadline, so what is on the machine is what you sent. Open it, look at the first slide, and confirm it is yours and that it renders. A file that was fine on your laptop is not the same as a file that is fine on theirs.
  4. Stand where you will be standing. Look at your own poster from three seconds away. That is the last useful thing you can do about it, and it tells you which sentence to lead with when somebody walks up.

Nothing here can be fixed after 9:00 AM, which is the reason the deadline is hard rather than a formality. Setup is confirmation, not preparation.

One-minute presentations, in order — both halves

The one-minute talks run in two blocks with the room reset between them. You present in an assigned slot, taken from what you submitted, and the timing is enforced.

What one minute means in practice:

  • Roughly 130 to 150 words. That is two sentences of finding and two of why it matters. It is not a compressed version of your poster.
  • Lead with the finding. At this length there is no room for a delayed answer — if you spend the first fifteen seconds on background, a third of your slot is gone before anyone knows what you did.
  • One purpose: make somebody walk to your poster. The talk is an invitation, not the argument. Leave them a reason to come and ask.
  • You will be cut off. Rehearse to fifty seconds so that being nervous does not cost you your last sentence.

Watch the second half if you are in the first, and the first half if you are in the second. Sixty seconds is a hard constraint and you are about to see twenty people solve it differently; which openings made you want to walk over is the most concentrated feedback on presentation structure you will get all semester.

The poster session, and what to write down before you leave

You stand with your work and talk to whoever comes. Part way through, visitors from outside the university arrive — that is when the audience stops being classmates and becomes strangers, and it is where the useful questions come from.

Write questions down in the hall, while you are standing there. Not afterward. Use your phone if that is what you have.

Three things to capture, because all three are graded later inside your final presentation:

  • The question you could not answer. In the words it was asked, not summarized.
  • The thing you had to explain twice. Twice means your framing is the problem, not the listener.
  • What somebody told you that you disagree with. Disagreeing is allowed and it has to be argued, which means you need their actual objection.

There is no debrief session. By December you will not remember which question was the good one, so the note you make in the hall is the whole record.

Before you arrive

  • Everything is due at 9:00 AM and that deadline is hard. Slides, poster file, competition submission. A late submission is not presented.
  • Know your slot time and be there before it.
  • Be able to say what you did in one sentence, then in thirty seconds, then in three minutes.

What this sets up

Write it down before you leave the hall. Every question you could not answer here becomes part of your final presentation, where what a public audience did not understand — and what you changed because of it — is graded. There is no debrief session to jog your memory two days later, and by December you will not remember which question was the good one.
