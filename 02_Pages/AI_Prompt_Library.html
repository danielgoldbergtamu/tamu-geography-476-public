AI Prompt Library

AI Toolkit · GEOG 476 · Bookmark this page

 

These are starting points, not scripts. Every one of them will work better if you adapt it to your actual situation — name your dataset, name your audience, paste in the real constraint. A prompt copied verbatim gets you a generic answer, which is the thing we are trying to teach you to avoid.

Four rules that matter more than any individual prompt

  1. Form your own view first. On anything you will be judging — a peer draft, your own argument, a result — write down what you think before you open a model. Once you read the AI's take, you are anchored to it.
  2. Give it something to push against. “Improve this” gets you mush. “Find the three weakest claims and say why each fails” gets you work.
  3. Ask it to be specific, and reject it when it isn't. If a critique could apply to any paper, it applies to none.
  4. Keep what you rejected. The critiques you overruled, and why, are the most valuable part of your AI Review Log — and the part that shows you were actually thinking.

The core three — use these on everything

Hard Reviewer

Finds weak reasoning before your instructor or your client does.

You are a demanding reviewer for a professional GIS journal. You are not
here to encourage me.

Read the draft below and identify its THREE WEAKEST claims. For each:
  1. Quote the exact sentence.
  2. State precisely why it fails - unsupported, overclaimed, circular,
     or contradicted elsewhere in the piece.
  3. State what evidence would be needed to make it hold.

Do not compliment the work. Do not soften your assessment. If the whole
argument rests on something unstated, say so first.

Non-Technical Reviewer

Your client's leadership will read this. They are smart. They are not GIS people.

You are an intelligent, senior decision-maker with no GIS background and
no time. You will act on this document, or not, based on one read.

Read the draft below and tell me:
  1. What you think it is claiming, in one sentence, in your own words.
  2. Every place you got lost, stopped, or had to re-read.
  3. Every term you did not understand.
  4. Whether you now know what you are supposed to DO. If not, say so.

Do not guess at meaning to be helpful. If it is unclear, report it as
unclear.

Non-GIS Reviewer

Catches the assumptions you cannot see because you are inside the field.

You are a sharp researcher from a completely different discipline -
economics, public health, journalism. You reason well. You know nothing
about geospatial methods.

Read the draft below and identify:
  1. Every assumption the author treats as obvious that is not obvious
     to you.
  2. Every methodological choice that is stated but never justified.
  3. Anywhere the author says something is 'standard' or 'well-known'
     without telling you why it is appropriate HERE.
  4. Any conclusion that does not follow from what came before.

Peer review prompts

Order of operations

Read your classmate’s draft and write down your own reactions before you run any of these. Then run them. Then compare. Your review is graded on what you add beyond the AI and where you correctly overrule it — neither of which you can demonstrate if you read the AI first.

Peer Draft — Structured Critique

Run this AFTER you have formed your own view. Step 2 of the protocol.

You are reviewing a classmate's draft in a senior GIS capstone. Your job
is to help them improve it, not to grade it and not to rewrite it.

Identify:
  1. The single most important thing that would improve this draft.
  2. Three specific weaknesses, each quoted and each with a concrete
     suggested fix.
  3. Two things that genuinely work and should be preserved - be
     specific, not encouraging.
  4. Anything that reads as generic - text that could appear in any
     student's draft on any topic.

ASSIGNMENT BRIEF:
[paste the assignment requirements]

CLASSMATE'S DRAFT:
[paste their draft]

Gap Finder — what did I miss?

Step 3. Turns the AI on your own review rather than on the draft.

Below are (A) a classmate's draft and (B) the review I wrote of it
before consulting you.

Tell me:
  1. What significant issues in the draft did my review MISS entirely?
  2. Where is my review vague where it should be specific?
  3. Where have I asserted something is wrong without explaining why?
  4. Is anything in my review actually incorrect about this draft?

Do not simply re-review the draft. Review MY REVIEW.

[paste their draft]

B) MY REVIEW:

Challenge the AI — where is it wrong?

Step 3, the critical half. This is where your grade actually lives.

Below is (A) a classmate's draft and (B) a critique of it produced by an
AI model.

Argue AGAINST that critique. For each point it raises:
  - Is it actually true of this specific draft, or is it a generic
    criticism that would apply to almost anything?
  - Does it misread what the author was attempting?
  - Would acting on it make the draft WORSE?
  - Does it reflect a real standard in this field, or an invented one?

Be genuinely adversarial. I am trying to find out which of these
critiques I should ignore.

[paste their draft]

B) AI CRITIQUE TO CHALLENGE:
[paste the critique you received]

Constructive Reframe

Step 4. For when your critique is right but lands badly.

Below is a critique I intend to give a classmate. The substance is
correct and I am not softening it.

Rewrite it so that it is:
  - Directed at the WORK, never at the person
  - Specific enough to act on - each point should name what to change
  - Ordered by importance, not by where it appears in the draft
  - Free of hedging that obscures the point ('maybe consider possibly')

Do not remove any criticism. Do not add praise that I did not write.
Keep it the same length or shorter.

Writing and revision prompts

Overclaim Detector

The fastest way to lose a technical reader is to claim more than you showed.

Read the draft below. Find every sentence that claims MORE than the
evidence presented actually supports.

For each one:
  1. Quote it.
  2. State what it claims.
  3. State what the evidence in this document actually supports.
  4. Rewrite it to match the evidence.

Pay particular attention to causal language ('leads to', 'drives',
'because of') where only correlation was demonstrated.

[paste your draft]

Generic Text Hunter

Finds the sentences that could have been written about anything.

Read the draft below. Identify every passage that is GENERIC - text that
could appear, essentially unchanged, in a paper on a completely
different topic.

Quote each one and tell me what specific, concrete detail from MY
project should replace it.

Be ruthless. Introductions and conclusions are usually the worst
offenders.

[paste your draft]

Structure Auditor

For when a draft has good content in the wrong order.

Read the draft below. Ignore the prose quality entirely - I only want
structure.

  1. Give me a one-line summary of what each paragraph is DOING
     (claiming, evidencing, transitioning, digressing).
  2. Identify any paragraph doing two jobs at once.
  3. Identify anything in the wrong place.
  4. Identify anything the argument needs that is missing.
  5. Propose a better order, and say why.

[paste your draft]

Opposing Advocate

For ethics essays and any piece taking a position.

I have argued the position below. Argue the OPPOSITE, as forcefully and
as well as you can. Use the strongest version of the counter-argument,
not a weak one that is easy to knock down.

Then tell me which of my claims that counter-argument damages most.

If you produce an argument I have not addressed in my draft, say so
explicitly - that is the gap I need to fix.

Skeptical Principal Investigator (S01)

Attacks your framing before you commit fifteen weeks to it.

You are a senior principal investigator - the person who leads a research project and answers for it - with thirty years in applied
geospatial work and no patience for vague projects.

Below is a client brief and my proposed component within it. Tell me:
  1. What is the actual question here? State it in one sentence.
  2. What would make this component USELESS to the client?
  3. What am I assuming is true that I have not checked?
  4. Is this achievable in fourteen weeks by one student? If not, what
     is the smaller version that is?
  5. If this component succeeds completely, what decision does it help
     someone make? If you cannot answer that, say so.

Data Quality Auditor (S04)

Fitness for use is where most student projects quietly fail.

You are a data quality auditor. I intend to use the dataset described
below for the analysis described below.

Interrogate my fitness-for-use reasoning:
  1. CURRENCY - is it recent enough for this question? How do I know?
  2. RESOLUTION - spatial, temporal, thematic. Adequate for the claim
     I want to make?
  3. COMPLETENESS - what is systematically missing, and does that bias
     my result in a predictable direction?
  4. PROVENANCE - who made it, how, and what were they optimizing for?
  5. LICENSE - may I use it this way and publish the result?
  6. What would make this dataset the WRONG choice here?

DATASET:
[describe it - source, date, resolution, coverage]

[describe it]

Project Manager personas

For the Project Manager. Management failure modes are not analytical failure modes, so the standard personas do not reach them.

Devil’s Advocate PM

One question, asked properly. Ask it every week.

Below is my project plan, component breakdown, and current status.

Answer one question: WHAT IS THE MOST LIKELY WAY THIS PROJECT FAILS?

Not a list of generic risks. The single most probable failure, named
specifically, with:
  - which component or dependency it starts in
  - what the earliest observable warning sign would be
  - how many weeks of warning I would realistically get
  - what I should be doing NOW to prevent or prepare for it

Then give me the second and third most likely, briefly.

[paste your latest status]

Risk Auditor

Kills the generic risks that make a risk register worthless.

Below is my risk register.

For each risk, tell me:
  1. Is this SPECIFIC to my project, or generic boilerplate that could
     appear on any project's register? Mark generic ones for deletion.
  2. Is the stated warning sign something that would actually be
     NOTICED, by a named person, in time to act?
  3. Is the mitigation real, or is it a restatement of the risk?
  4. What risk is MISSING that this project's structure creates?

Be harsh about generic entries. 'Scope creep' and 'resource
constraints' are not risks, they are words.

Technical GIS prompts — for the work, not the writing

Everything above points AI at what you wrote. These point it at what you did. They are what the AI Method Log records, and the log asks one thing above all others: how did you verify it?

Before you use any of these

Every one of these prompts has a place to paste your actual data description. Use it. A model told nothing about your data answers about someone else’s.

You may not adopt a method you cannot defend independently of the tool that suggested it. Check it against the documentation, a known-good result, a hand calculation, or a test that would fail. “It ran” is not a check, and asking the same model twice is not a second opinion.

Source Finder

Names candidate datasets. Half of what it names will not exist — that is the point of the next prompt.

I need data for the following analysis. Name up to eight candidate
datasets.

For each one give me: the publishing organization, the actual dataset
name as that organization calls it, spatial resolution or scale,
temporal coverage, license, and how it is obtained.

Mark any entry you are not confident actually exists under that name.
Do not give me a URL unless you are certain of it.

ANALYSIS:
[what you are trying to measure, where, at what resolution]

CONSTRAINTS:
[license, cost, currency, coverage you must have]

Source Verifier

Run immediately after Source Finder. This is the verification step, and it is not optional.

Below is a list of datasets you named for my analysis.

For each one, tell me exactly what I should type into a search engine
or which catalog I should open to confirm it exists, and what I should
expect to see if it does.

Then tell me which entries in this list you are LEAST confident about
and why.

I am going to open every one of these. Anything you invented will be
found in the next ten minutes, so tell me now.

LIST:
[paste what it gave you]

Projection Adviser

Ask BEFORE you reproject. Say what you are measuring, or you will be given Web Mercator.

I need to choose a coordinate reference system.

MY DATA: [geometry type, extent in degrees, current CRS]
WHAT I AM MEASURING: [area / distance / direction / shape / none of
  these, just display]
WHO USES THE OUTPUT: [and whether they expect a particular CRS]

Recommend a CRS and give me:
  - the EPSG code, and what its parameters mean for MY extent
  - the property it preserves and the property it sacrifices
  - the approximate magnitude of the distortion across my extent
  - a concrete way I can CHECK your recommendation myself, using a
    quantity I can look up independently

If more than one CRS is defensible, say so and give me the trade-off.

Geoprocessing Explainer

For when two tools sound identical and are not.

In [ArcGIS Pro / QGIS / GDAL], I want to [describe the operation in
plain language, with your data].

Tell me:
  1. Which tool does this, and its exact name in that software.
  2. What EVERY parameter means, in terms of my data, not in general.
  3. The default value of each, and whether the default is right here.
  4. At least one other tool that sounds like it does the same thing,
     and precisely how the result would differ.
  5. What the output looks like if I get a parameter wrong in a way
     that does NOT throw an error.

State which software version you are describing. If a parameter
changed between versions, say so.

MY DATA:
[geometry, CRS, feature count, attribute of interest]

Script Drafter

For repetitive steps. The output is a draft to be read, not a result to be trusted.

Draft a [Python / arcpy / GDAL command line] script for the following
repetitive step.

Requirements:
  - Comment every line that makes an assumption about my data.
  - Fail loudly rather than silently on unexpected input.
  - Tell me which library versions this depends on, and flag anything
    deprecated.
  - At the end, list the three things most likely to go wrong when I
    run this, and what each would look like.

TASK:
[what you are automating]

MY DATA:
[format, CRS, size, naming convention]

Three Causes Debugger

Ask for three. Fixating on the first plausible cause is how an afternoon disappears.

I have an error. Do not give me one answer.

Give me the THREE most likely causes, ordered by how cheap they are to
rule out. For each: what to check, what I would see if that is the
cause, and what I would see if it is not.

SOFTWARE AND VERSION:
[...]

WHAT I RAN:
[the tool and parameters, or the code]

ERROR, VERBATIM:
[paste it, all of it]

MY DATA:
[format, CRS, size, anything unusual about it]

Result Falsifier

The most useful and least used prompt on this page. Run it before you believe your own output.

Below is a result I have produced. Your job is to help me find out
whether it is WRONG. Do not tell me it looks reasonable.

Tell me:
  1. What would I check to know this is wrong?
  2. What does this method handle badly, and does my data contain
     those cases? Name them.
  3. What would this result look like if I had made the most common
     error for this method — would I be able to tell?
  4. What independent quantity could I compare this against?
  5. Is the magnitude plausible? Show me the arithmetic.

METHOD:
[what you did, step by step]

RESULT:
[the numbers, with units]

DATA:
[extent, resolution, feature count, CRS]

Classification Critic

For choropleths. An indefensible classification is an indefensible claim.

I am mapping the attribute below and have chosen a classification.

Tell me:
  1. Is this classification defensible for THIS distribution? Why or
     why not?
  2. What impression does it create that a different, equally
     defensible choice would not?
  3. Which class breaks are doing the most work in the reader's
     conclusion?
  4. Would a reader who disagreed with my conclusion attack this
     choice? What would they say?

DISTRIBUTION:
[min, max, median, skew, n, and any zeros or outliers]

CLASSIFICATION:
[method, number of classes, the breaks]

WHAT THE MAP IS FOR:
[the decision it supports]

Schedule Breaker

Project Managers: the technical prompt for the work you own.

Below is my project schedule and component breakdown.

Tell me:
  1. If ONE component slips by a week, which one causes the most
     damage, and what breaks downstream?
  2. Which dependencies are real but unstated in this plan?
  3. Where are two people implicitly waiting on each other?
  4. What is on the critical path that nobody has called critical?

Use MY component names. If you find yourself writing generic project
management advice, stop and re-read the plan.

[paste it]

COMPONENTS AND OWNERS:
[paste them]

Recording these in your AI Method Log

Naming the prompt is not enough. The Method Log wants the stage of the work, the prompt with your context in it, what it proposed, how you verified it, and what it got wrong. The Source Finder and Source Verifier pair is the easiest place to produce a real “what it got wrong” entry — it will name a dataset that does not exist, and you will be the one who found out.

Using these in your AI Review Log

Every assignment requires an AI Review Log. Which prompts you ran and what you did with the results is the substance of it — especially the field asking what you rejected. Naming a persona is not enough; record what it actually said and what you did about it.
