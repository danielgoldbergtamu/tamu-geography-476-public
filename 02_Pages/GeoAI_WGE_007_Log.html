Slide 9 of the client plan deck. It is currently a SPECIMEN: the output form of an answer, drawn with stand-in values, stamped as such on its face. The task is to make it real.

1. What the existing specimen promised, and what that commits us to

The plan register already states the method for SQ3, written before any of this was attempted:

Harmonize US county and Mexican municipio livestock census into one classification applied to both national censuses.

That sentence contains the whole difficulty, and it is worth being precise about it before opening a single dataset.

A choropleth of head counts by county would be the obvious map and it would be wrong. Counties and municipios differ in area by more than an order of magnitude across this region, so a map coloured by TOTAL HEAD is a map of how big the administrative units are. Density fixes that but introduces its own problem: a national census is collected under national definitions, on national boundaries, in different years, and the two sides do not join at the river.

So the question splits into two, and they have different answers:

  1. Where is the cattle mass, comparably, across the border? This needs one method applied to both countries, not two censuses stitched together.
  2. Which named units are the important producers? This needs the censuses themselves, because a modelled surface cannot tell you the name of a county.

3. What each pull cost, and one that needed a different trick

GLW4. The Dataverse SEARCH API is reachable by fetch from a page, which found doi:10.7910/DVN/LHBICE — Global cattle distribution in 2015 (5 minutes of arc). The dataset API then listed four rasters, and all four matter:

5_Ct_2015_Da.tif   10 MB  dasymetric  - the modelled distribution
6_Ct_2015_Aw.tif    6 MB  areal-weighted - the census spread flat over its own reporting unit
7_Ct_2015_Ps.tif    1 MB  prediction status - where the model extrapolated
8_Areakm.tif        2 MB  pixel area in km2

8_Areakm.tif is the one an unwary analyst skips and should not. The rasters are in degrees, so pixel area falls with latitude; head per pixel divided by a constant area is a density map with a latitude gradient baked into it that has nothing to do with cattle.

NASS. No API key needed by a route that is easy to miss: https://www.nass.usda.gov/datasets/ publishes the whole Quick Stats corpus as gzipped pipe files. qs.census2022.txt.gz is 309 MB, and one awk pass pulled out the 3,060 county rows that matter.

SIAP. The download link on the open-data page has no .csv in its href and two clicks on it produced nothing, so the page's own markup was read instead. The municipal file is served from an opaque endpoint:

https://nube.agricultura.gob.mx/index.php?view=E370DEBE-...&ANIO=2025

3.4 MB, 33,724 rows, 2,287 municipios, definitive 2025 figures.

4. The asymmetry nobody can fix, and what to do about it

The two censuses do not measure the same thing.

  • NASS gives INVENTORY — head standing on the ground, 2022, by county.
  • SIAP's cierre gives PRODUCTION — tonnes of meat, litres of milk, and Asacrificado, head slaughtered, 2025, by municipio.

A map that joined those two across the river would be comparing a stock to a flow, in different years, and colouring the join. This is exactly why the harmonized grid is the primary layer and the censuses are not joined at all. They name places and they check the grid; they do not get averaged together.

5. The check that makes the grid usable

GLW is a MODEL. Before drawing anything, it was tested against the two national censuses it was not built from — GLW is 2015, the US census is 2022:

Texas    GLW4 12,019,818   vs   NASS 2022 census 12,543,300     -4.2%
Mexico   GLW4 33,478,560   vs   national inventory ~33-35 million

Four percent apart across a seven-year gap. The grid is good enough to carry the pattern, and the number is on the figure so a reader can decide that for themselves rather than take it on trust.

6. What the data says, before any map is drawn

The question as asked is underspecified, and the choice of measure moves the answer five hundred miles. Three defensible readings of "important cattle-producing areas" in Texas, from the same census:

All cattle and calves   Deaf Smith 700,069   Castro 582,951   Hartley 420,346   -> the Panhandle
Cattle on feed          Deaf Smith 364,275   Castro 316,700   Hartley 254,889   -> the Panhandle
Beef cows (the herd)    Lavaca 68,262        Houston 63,017   Fayette 57,925    -> East and Central Texas

The first two are feedlots — animals in confinement, hundreds of miles from the border. The third is the cow-calf herd on extensive rangeland, and for a screwworm question it is the one that matters: those are the animals outdoors, exposed, and moved.

In Mexico, 9,091,841 head were slaughtered in 2025 and the mass sits in Veracruz (1,156,903), Jalisco (935,046) and Durango (547,750). The four states on the US border account for 12.1% of the national total. Veracruz is also the state carrying 4,769 screwworm cases.

And the finding the client will actually use. Share of each country's cattle within a band of the international line:

The Mexican side is roughly two and a half times more cattle-dense near the border than the Texas side, at every band — and neither country keeps its herd there. The important producing areas are inland on both sides. Which means the risk geography for this project is not where the cattle are; it is the route between them, which is what slides 10 and 13 already map.

7. The map: a hexagonal grid, and why not a choropleth

Dan asked for "a heatmap over a grid representation". A hexagonal bin is the right grid for this and the reason is worth stating:

  • A choropleth of head by county is a map of how big the counties are. Units across this region differ in area by more than an order of magnitude.
  • Hexagons are equal-area and have no orientation bias, so a dark cell means density and nothing else.
  • The binning is done on projected coordinates in an Albers equal-area conic. Binning in degrees would make the northern hexes smaller than the southern ones and put a latitude gradient into the answer.

Each hexagon covers about 2,436 km², and 60,665 GLW cells carrying 68,160,449 cattle fall inside the frame.

7.1 A framing error caught by looking at the render

The first frame ran to 33.5 degrees north. Deaf Smith, Castro and Hartley — the three counties the census says hold the most cattle in Texas — sit above 34.5 and were outside it. A map titled "where the cattle are" that excludes the largest concentration in the state is not a small framing error, and no checker would have said a word. Found by looking at the picture. The frame now runs to 37.6.

7.2 A phantom error, and the shape of the mistake

The density depends entirely on one number: the area of a hexagon. Get it wrong and every value on the legend is wrong by the same factor, silently.

The first derivation took the hexagon's bounding box from hb.get_paths() and applied the regular-hexagon formula, giving 2,435.5 km². To check it, a uniform point cloud was binned over the frame with the same gridsize and the cells counted: 2,873 km². An 18 percent disagreement, apparently in the map.

The map was right and the check was wrong. hexbin sizes its lattice to whatever data it is handed. The calibration cloud filled the whole map FRAME; the real data only occupies the land that has cattle on it, which is a smaller rectangle. So the calibration measured bigger hexagons than the figure actually draws.

Re-run over the same extent as the real data, the two agree:

lattice formula sx*sy/2      2,435.5 km2
measured, 10% margin         2,380.1 km2
measured, 15% margin         2,500.3 km2
measured, 20% margin         2,389.3 km2

This is the exact mirror image of the failure recorded in F171. There, a verification that SHARED the fix's assumptions reported a phantom success. Here, a verification run against a DIFFERENT setup from the thing it was checking reported a phantom failure. Both come from the same root: the check has to exercise the artifact, under the conditions the artifact actually runs in.

The wrong explanation nearly got written into the source as a comment claiming an 8 percent bug that never existed. A false explanation in the code is worse than no explanation, because the next reader believes it.
