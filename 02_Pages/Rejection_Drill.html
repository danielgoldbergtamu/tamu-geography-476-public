The Rejection Drill

A ten-minute exercise we run in the first class. This page is the instructions, the material, and the criteria

 

A ten-minute exercise we run in the first class. This page is the instructions, the material, and the criteria you are graded against for the rest of the term — in that order. If you missed the class, everything you need to do it on your own is here.

What you are being asked to do

Below are six AI suggestions — the numbered points an AI assistant gave in a single answer about analyzing incident points for a client. Some of it is wrong. Some of it is correct. You find what you would reject, and you write down why.

The reason is the whole exercise. Spotting that something is wrong is worth almost nothing on its own — anyone can be suspicious. What is assessed, here and for the rest of the course, is whether you can say what is wrong in a way somebody else could go and check.

Step by step

  1. Read the six AI suggestions. About a minute. Do not start judging yet, just read them.
  2. On your own — four minutes. Go through them one at a time. For each one you would reject, write a single line saying what is wrong with it. Number your lines to match the AI suggestions. Four minutes across six suggestions is forty seconds each, and that is deliberate — at work you will not get ten minutes to think about one sentence. If you run short, pick one and do it properly rather than writing six lines that say "wrong".
  3. In pairs — two minutes. Compare your lists with the person next to you. Where you disagree, the question to ask is not who is right — it is whose reason could be checked.
  4. Whole room — five minutes. Some of you read yours out. Then you see what was actually wrong with each sentence, and the five kinds of reason you can give.

Two rules while you work. You may look anything up — checking a claim is the skill being trained, so use whatever you would use at work. You may not ask an AI whether the AI was right; that is the move this whole course exists to replace.

You are not expected to find all of them. You are also not expected to reject all of them, and that matters more than it sounds — see the mistake worth making below.

The prompt, and the six AI suggestions

This is what was asked. A client handed over a spreadsheet of incident points and wanted a map of where they cluster; this is the whole of what went to the AI assistant, typed the way anybody types it when they are busy.

I have 4,300 incident points for the client's service area and I need to show where they cluster. What should I do?

And this is what came back. Six numbered points, in one answer.

  1. The Universal Transverse Mercator system divides the world into 60 zones, each 6 degrees of latitude wide, so pick the zone your study area falls in.
  2. Always state the coordinate reference system you used, because a reader cannot check a distance without it.
  3. Aggregate your incident points to census tracts rather than counties — because tracts are smaller, the density estimate will be more accurate.
  4. Use a 500-meter buffer, which is the standard for accessibility analysis.
  5. Keep the write-up non-technical, since your audience will not have a GIS background.
  6. Report your results to three decimal places to show precision.

The prompt is bad on purpose, and that is the first thing worth noticing. It does not say what the incidents are, what the service area looks like, who the map is for, or what decision it has to support — so the assistant filled every one of those gaps with an assumption, silently, and six suggestions came back built on assumptions nobody saw it make. That is the ordinary case, not a trick.

The habit that prevents most of it is one line, and it goes at the top of the first message every time:

"If you do not know what I mean or something is missing, ask me rather than guessing."

A tool that asks you two questions first will beat a tool that answers immediately, nearly always. More on this on the How to talk to one of these page.

What makes a rejection defensible

Three tests. A rejection that fails any of them is an opinion, and opinions are worth less.

  • Specific. You name which AI suggestion, and what in it is wrong. "AI suggestion 3 is wrong because smaller units change the statistic" passes. "I disagreed with this one" does not.
  • Checkable. Somebody else could go and find out whether you are right. If it cannot be checked, what you have is a preference — which is allowed, and is worth less.
  • About the work. The reason refers to the subject matter or to this client, not to how the sentence reads. Style is the weakest reason available to you.

What a good rejection looks like

The same objection to AI suggestion 3, written three ways. Only one of them is worth points.

What a good rejection looks like
Worth nothing"AI suggestion 3 is wrong." No reason. Nobody can check it — including you, in three weeks, when you have to defend it.
Worth little"I don't think census tracts are the right unit here." A preference. It may even be correct, and nothing in it can be verified, so it cannot be graded as judgment.
What you are aiming for"AI suggestion 3 says smaller units are more accurate. They are not — aggregating the same points to tracts instead of counties changes the density values, which is the modifiable areal unit problem. The unit should match the area this client acts on, not the smallest one available."

Walk the third one against the three tests above and you can see why it earns what it earns. It says which AI suggestion. It says what is wrong with it. The claim about the modifiable areal unit problem can be checked by anyone who looks it up. And the last sentence is about this client rather than about GIS in general.

What is not being asked for is that length, or that vocabulary. One line is enough. What is being asked for is that the line survive somebody checking it.

The five kinds of reason

You are graded on which of these you give, all term, and not on how many rejections you make. They are listed in descending order of what they are worth.

The five kinds of reason
KindWhat it isWhy it is worth what it is
FactualThe tool asserted something untrue about the subject.It requires you to be right about the domain, not merely confident.
MethodologicalThe advice is valid in general and wrong for this case.The hardest one. You have to know where a good method stops applying.
Audience or purposeThe critique is sound but assumes a different reader or goal.Often caused by your own prompt — and saying so is worth points, not points lost.
PreferenceYou would have done it differently, and you are not claiming an error.Legitimate. Worth little, because nobody can check it.
No reason given"I disagreed."Worth nothing. It is also the most common thing students write.

Counting rejections measures compliance. The reason you give measures competence. That sentence is the whole design of how AI use is assessed in this course, and it is why the rubric rewards the warrant rather than the count.

The mistake worth making

One of the six AI suggestions is correct. If you reject it, that is the most useful thing you do in those ten minutes.

You were told some of the advice was wrong, so you went looking for errors and you found them — including where there was not one. A tool that is wrong sometimes is more dangerous than one that is wrong always, because it teaches you to stop reading carefully in both directions. Over-rejection is a failure of judgment in exactly the same way that accepting everything is, and it is the harder one to notice in yourself.

Where this goes

  • Every draft you submit goes past an AI reviewer first, and you record what it said and what you rejected.
  • Field 5 of the AI Review Log — what you rejected, and why — carries more weight than any other field in it.
  • The knowledge check, taken in week one and again in week fifteen, is where you see whether this changed.

None of that is a separate assignment. It is how the work in this course is done.

If you missed the class

Do it on your own, in the same order: four minutes alone, then bring your list to the next session and compare it with somebody.

Write your lines before you read past the six sentences. The sections below give part of it away — the mistake worth making tells you how many of the six are actually wrong — and reading that first turns a ten-minute exercise into nothing at all.
