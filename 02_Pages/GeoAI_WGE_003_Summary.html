What the data turned out to be

Every source that was tried, including the ones that gave nothing. A source that yielded nothing is a finding, not a gap in the record.

NASA Earth Observatory, "Nairobi Swells with Urban Growth"gave part of it — The pair, the sensors and the dates: Landsat 5 TM on 5 January 1986 and Landsat 8 OLI on 24 January 2016, plus the density figure of roughly 60% of the population on 6% of the land. The first sentence decided how the figure had to be drawn; the second decided what the slide had to say.

What does not exist

  • A scale for either figure. NASA publishes these as rendered JPEGs with no stated extent, and the georeferenced GeoTIFF sits on the same host the network refuses. A scale bar drawn from a guess at the frame width would be an invented measurement, so both figures state its absence instead of showing one.
  • Any delineation of the built-up edge. Nothing here measures anything; these are pictures of a city at two dates. The measurement is the project's first component and the slides say so.

What was NOT built, and why

Read this section first if you read nothing else. The deliverable that was refused is the one you would most likely have produced.

  • Placing the Nairobi pair as published, side by side. The 2016 frame is 4000 x 4000 and the 1986 frame is 2000 x 2000 over the same ground. A sharper picture of a city reads as more city, so the published pair overstates the growth it is being used to show — the exact confound the project's own imagery component names.
  • Built instead: The generator resamples both frames to the coarser grid before drawing them, and the figure states that it did.
  • Drawing a scale bar. Nothing in the JPEG says how wide the frame is, and the georeferenced version could not be fetched. A plausible-looking scale bar is a measurement claim, and an invented measurement is worse than a missing one.
  • Built instead: A "what this figure does not have" line, on the face of the figure.

What no check would have caught

The rules and gates this course runs on, and why each one would have passed the version that was wrong.

  • looking at the finished slide — the resampling is invisible once it is done, and so is not doing it
  • any check in this build, none of which open a JPEG or compare two frames' pixel dimensions
  • a reader who trusts that two images side by side are comparable because they are side by side

What went wrong while building it

  • The composed figure was written with its title, captions and caveats in the Python file. Speed. It is the fastest way to get a figure on screen and it puts course prose where no linter, no accessibility check and no editor who is not a programmer can reach it. Caught by Writing it into content/projects/urbanization_imagery.yaml instead, before the first render — the same correction the W02 figure needed two hours earlier, made without waiting for the ratchet to fail..
  • The first render put the subtitle through the date labels, overflowed the caveat box, and printed the NASA credit on top of the last line of the footnote. Fixed row positions in a footer whose content varies in length. Caught by Looking at the rendered PNG. Nothing raised an error; matplotlib will happily draw text on top of text..
  • The first deck version repeated the figure's own title as the slide title, costing the picture two inches of height. Reusing the standard slide header without asking what the figure already carried. Caught by Reading the rendered PDF page..

What to take from it

  • Find the data before designing the analysis. Thirty seconds of testing whether an image could be downloaded reordered the entire task.
  • Two images side by side are a comparison, and a comparison is a claim that everything except the thing being compared is held constant. Pixel dimensions, season, haze and sun angle are all "everything else".
  • Resolution is the confound nobody sees. Four times the pixels over the same ground looks like more city, and the published pair invites exactly that reading.
  • Provenance survives renaming badly. nairobi_tm5_1986005 carries the sensor and the acquisition day-of-year; nairobi_before.jpg carries nothing.
  • State what the figure does not have. No scale bar, no projection, no delineation — written on the figure, where a reader who is about to misuse it will see it.
  • Public domain is not the same as reachable. These are US government works, free to use, and the network between here and them said no.

What this means for the job you are about to apply for

The part of this an entry-level analyst would be asked to do — find a credible before/after pair, get it, put it on a slide — took half an hour including the fights with the network. The part that is worth paying for is the half-page of caveats: knowing that the 2016 frame is sharper and saying so, refusing the scale bar, and labeling the cities as examples because the client's city list has not arrived. That judgment is the job. The retrieval is not.

The map itself

Shown here rather than only linked, because a map is the one artifact where seeing it IS understanding it.

What this map shows Two Landsat images of the same frame around Nairobi, Kenya, side by side, drawn at the same display resolution so neither looks sharper than the other. On the left, 5 January 1986, Landsat 5: the built-up area is a compact pale gray-pink patch near the center, with dark forested highland to the north-west and dry open country to the south and east. On the right, 24 January 2016, Landsat 8: the pale area has spread outward in every direction except southward, where it stops along a dark wedge - Nairobi National Park - and smaller pale settlements now sit along the roads leading away from the city. A column at the right gives what to look at and the population and density figures. A panel across the bottom states, in bold, that the 2016 frame is published at four times the pixel count of the 1986 frame over the same ground and has been resampled down to match, and that the two Januaries differ in haze and greenness so color differences are not all change. It also states what the figure does not have: no scale bar, because the published JPEG carries no extent; no stated projection, because these are NASA's renderings; and no delineation of the built-up edge. The figure establishes that the city expanded greatly over thirty years, and that two of the differences a viewer can see - sharpness and color - are properties of the imagery rather than of the city.

What this map shows Two Landsat images of the same frame around Benin City, Nigeria, side by side at identical pixel dimensions, so nothing has been resampled. On the left, 28 January 2002, Landsat 7: a gray built-up mass at the center with roads radiating outward and vegetated land still filling the wedges between them. On the right, 11 January 2025, Landsat 9: the gray has extended along every radiating road and filled much of the land between them. A column at the right gives what to look at and the reported figures - developed land nearly doubled, vegetated area down by more than 650 square kilometers, population from about one million to over two million. A panel across the bottom states that both frames are the same pixel dimensions over the same ground, so no sharpness difference is doing any of the work, and lists what the figure does not have: no scale bar, no stated projection, and no delineation of the built-up edge. The figure establishes both edge expansion along corridors and infill between them - two of the three growth types the project asks students to separate and measure.

A vector PDF of the same figure is in the table below. Download that one if you need to zoom — the panels draw some counties a few millimeters across and a magnified PNG turns to mush.
