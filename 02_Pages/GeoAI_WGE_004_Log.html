2. The first thing to establish: is there a file?

There is not. The paste arrives as something I can LOOK at — I can read "PASSAU (1838)", the neighbouring-sheet names in the margins, the graticule labels, the Bayerische Staatsbibliothek stamp and the scale statement — and it is not a file on any disk I can reach. Nothing to open, nothing to measure pixels in, nothing to write a transform onto.

Checked /mnt/user-data/uploads: only artifacts from earlier today. Checked _Inbox on his machine, where his browser saves: not there either.

This is worth stating plainly for students, because it is counter-intuitive. Seeing an image and having an image are different things, and only the second one can be georeferenced. The same distinction bit us in WGE-003 in the opposite direction: there I could read the page describing the imagery and not download the imagery.

Even if the paste had produced a file, it would be the wrong file. A chat paste is a screen-resolution rendering. Georeferencing a downsampled JPEG produces a georeferenced downsampled JPEG — fine for throwing on a web map, useless for measuring anything at 1:50,000, which is the whole reason to georeference a topographic sheet rather than screenshot it.

3. What the sheet itself says, read off the paste

Read from the image before touching any tool:

  • Title PASSAU (1838), sheet number 65 at the top right.
  • Neighbouring sheets named in the margins: Osterhofen (north), Landau (west), Seitzhalmünster? / Griesbach (south edge lettering).
  • A graticule labelled along the top and bottom in degrees and minutes running 1°25', 1°30', 1°35', 1°40', 1°45', 1°50' — far too small to be Greenwich or Ferro.
  • Latitude ticks at the left and right edges in the high 48s.
  • Scale statement: "Eine Chaussée Meile zu 25406 bayer. Fuß" — one highway mile of 25,406 Bavarian feet, not a metric scale bar.
  • The eastern edge of the sheet is the Erzherzogthum Österreich boundary, running with the Inn past Passau and Schärding.

The longitude reading was checked against the catalogue rather than assumed. The Deutsche Digitale Bibliothek record for the atlas states it outright:

"Maßstab in graph. Form (Chaussée-Meile). - Nullmeridian: München."

So the graticule is degrees east of the Munich meridian, and the sheet's 1°50' column falls where Passau is, which is right: Passau sits about 1°50' east of Munich. That single fact decides the whole transform, and it was available in a library catalogue in thirty seconds.

6. Finding the graticule, and getting it wrong twice

The plan was: find the neat line, find the graticule ticks on it, read their labels, build control points at the intersections.

First attempt found only the frame corners. The bands I sampled were in the wrong place — I had guessed the border's anatomy instead of measuring it.

Second attempt found nothing either, for a different reason: the ticks are not black. Printing a vertical slice of pixel values through a column where a tick was visible showed the tick as mid-grey, between 130 and 195, against a frame that is solid black below 130. The threshold that isolates the frame erases the graticule. Raising the ink threshold to 195 turned 2 detections into 12.

Border anatomy, once measured rather than assumed: thick black frame, thin rule, a blank label band where the tick lines and their numbers live, then the graduated minute band, then the map.

Ticks found, in full-resolution pixels:

TOP     x = 2138.0  4014.5  5878.5  7746.0  9614.0  11478.5
BOTTOM  x = 2313.5  4185.5  6064.5  7947.0  9821.0  11697.5
LEFT    y = 1368.5  4218.5  7057.5
RIGHT   y = 1013.5  3858.0  6710.5

Labels were read, not inferred. Crops at the ticks show 1°50' on the top border and — the one that mattered — 35' on BOTH the left and right borders.

7. Two things that looked like errors and were the projection

The top ticks sit ~175 px LEFT of the bottom ticks. That is meridian convergence: a meridian east of the central meridian leans back toward it going north. Predicted shift from Δλ·sin φ over the sheet height: ~148 px. Observed 175.

The same parallel is 360 px HIGHER at the east edge than at the west edge. That is the curvature of a parallel on a conic-family projection, arcs centred on the pole, and the sheet sits entirely east of the Munich central meridian so it catches only one limb of the arc. Same order as a rough Bonne calculation.

Neither is scan rotation, which the straight, square frame rules out. Both are what the map is supposed to look like, and either could have been "corrected" into a worse georeference by somebody who assumed the graticule should be a rectangle.

Because parallels are arcs and each has only two ticks, they are used as chords. The sag of a chord against the true arc, over this sheet, works out at ~11 px, about 36 m on the ground — stated rather than hidden.

8. The internal check that says the reading is right

18 control points, from 6 meridians x 3 parallels:

px per arc-minute:  longitude 373.6   latitude 568.9
ground metres per pixel:  x 3.28   y 3.26
implied scan resolution at 1:50,000:  x 387 dpi   y 390 dpi

Those two numbers agreeing is the check. Longitude and latitude are measured in different directions, from different ticks, with different labels; a misread label — 5' where the sheet says 10' — would throw x and y apart immediately. They agree to under 1%, and the implied scan resolution is a believable 387/390 dpi.

Second-order polynomial fit through the 18 points: RMS 1.5 px in x, 1.2 px in y, about 6 m on the ground. That is the internal precision of the graticule network and nothing more.

9. The constant that is a decision, not a measurement

The sheet gives longitude east of MUNICH. Converting to Greenwich needs a value for the Munich meridian, and there is more than one published answer:

A  Sternwarte Bogenhausen   11°36'32.24"
B  old city meridian        11°34'00"
C  Soldner origin           11°36'27.8"

A and C differ by ~90 m and are indistinguishable here. B differs from A by 2.5 arc-minutes, about 3.1 km, or 950 pixels — and it is testable. Predicting where Passau's Danube-Inn confluence should fall:

A -> pixel 12189, 4258     lands on the old town, Oberhaus above it,
                           Innstadt across the Inn
B -> pixel 13136, 4231     lands 58 px from the right frame, in the margin

B is dead. The town is not at the sheet's edge. A adopted, and recorded in the writing script so a different choice can be re-run.

10. What the absolute check actually shows

The warped raster was sampled at the modern coordinate of the Danube-Inn confluence (13.4696 E, 48.5725 N) with a crosshair drawn on it and a scale bar.

The crosshair lands on the southern edge of the old town, on the Inn bank. The 1838 sheet's Ortsspitze — the tip of the peninsula — is several hundred metres further east, past the right edge of a crop 405 m wide.

So: internal precision ~6 m, absolute agreement with modern coordinates several hundred metres, biased east. Four candidates, and this log does not claim to have separated them:

  1. The atlas used a Munich meridian value different from the one adopted.
  2. 1838 survey error in the sheet's own control.
  3. Real change at the confluence between 1838 and now.
  4. My identification of the feature on a hachured engraving.

An earlier eyeball estimate in this session put the offset at ~1 km. Measuring it against a drawn scale bar cut it to several hundred metres. The first number was mine, not the map's, and it is left in this log because that is the point: an accuracy figure produced by looking is not an accuracy figure.

Separating those four needs 8-12 stable, sharply identifiable check points — church towers, bridge abutments — digitised on both the sheet and a modern reference, then a least-squares solve. That is a real exercise and it is exactly the shape of the delineation-protocol work in the urbanization project.

13. The check, and the measurement error inside the measurement

Five towns, coordinates from an independent modern source, one panel each with a crosshair on the modern position.

The first pass was read by eye and it was wrong. It put Fürstenzell 240 m WEST while every other town was east — an outlier that would have wrecked the conclusion. The cause, found by drawing a labelled 100 m grid and looking again: on this atlas the place NAME is not drawn at the place. "Fürstenzell" is set in large letters spanning a kilometre of the sheet, and I had measured to the letters. The buildings are ~700 m east of where the label starts.

Re-measured against the grid:

Same direction, same size, no north component. An 1838 survey's error would scatter in direction as well as magnitude. A constant displacement along one axis is a coordinate-system difference: the graticule is drawn in a 19th-century Bavarian astronomic datum with its origin at Munich, and WGS84 is not that datum. The adopted meridian constant and the datum shift are not separable from four check points, and this log does not pretend to have separated them — the fitted number absorbs both.

14. Verified, not asserted

The fitted raster was re-sampled at the same towns' modern coordinates. The crosshairs now land on the settlements; residuals are roughly 100-200 m, which is the same order as my ability to say which building is "the town" on a hachured engraving. That is where this measurement stops being about the map and starts being about me.

17. A route tested and rejected: geocoding the hamlet names

The sheets name farmsteads — Hahberg, Kreiling, Aigenberg, Wesensandbach — so the obvious move is to look those up and use them as control.

"Aigenberg" resolved to a mountain peak in Lower Austria, 200 km away.

Lower Bavarian hamlet names repeat endlessly and a gazetteer will answer confidently every time. Feeding those answers in as control points would have produced a georeference that was precise, verifiable-looking and wrong. Rejected.

What did work was reading the map instead: a village in red script, Weßensandbach, on the north bank, with the road labelled Straubing to the west and nach Passau to the east, and Donau on the margin. That is Sandbach, Stadt Vilshofen an der Donau — the sheet is placed by its content, not by its names.

18. The scale, measured rather than assumed

The instructor added, mid-run:

(this is a meta note, for the logs. I do not want it to change how you are processing. Keep going as you had planned. The reported scale of these maps by their owner is supposed to be 1:5000, and you are currently assuming 1:25000 - I want to see how this plays out.)

Worth being exact about what was assumed. The georeference itself never takes a scale as input — a fit to control points RETURNS the scale. 1:25,000 had entered only as a sanity check on the sheet's physical size.

So it became a measurement. Two places on the sheet with independent modern coordinates:

Kreiling   48.628009 N  13.275862 E
Sandbach   48.606860 N  13.272460 E      ground separation 2363 m

On the sheet, read off a labelled pixel grid, their settlements are about 5400 pixels apart. At 600 dpi that is 228.6 mm of paper.

228.6 mm  <->  2363 m      =>  approximately 1:10,300
ground resolution           =>  0.44 m per pixel
sheet extent (7398 px)      =>  about 3.2 km square

That contradicts both stated scales. Not 1:25,000, which would require those two villages to be 5.7 km apart when they are 2.4 km apart. Not 1:5,000, which would need 11,164 pixels to hold them and the sheet is 7,398 pixels wide — the sheet physically could not contain both villages at 1:5,000.

A third, independent indicator agrees: the Danube is drawn about 700 px wide, which is 305 m at this scale — right for this reach. At 1:25,000 it would be 738 m wide.

What is NOT yet established, and it is the weak link: all of this rests on my reading of two handwritten hamlet names, and one cross-check already disagrees — Kreiling should be 250 m EAST of Sandbach and the sheet puts it 17 m west. That is either a rotation, or sloppy centres, or a misidentified village, and 2 points cannot tell those apart. Three or more unambiguous points can, and the 1838 sheet already georeferenced covers this same ground, which makes river bends — features no gazetteer can misname — available as control.

Recorded as a finding rather than resolved: the sheets are not at the scale their owner states, and they are not at the scale the assistant assumed either.

19. Using the georeferenced 1838 sheet as the bridge

The hamlet names could not be trusted to a gazetteer, so the 1838 sheet — already georeferenced, and covering this same ground — was used to read coordinates instead. It names everything the Positionsblatt names, and it corrected two of my readings immediately:

  • The red district name is "Boesen Sandbach" (Bösensandbach), not "Weßensandbach" as I had transcribed it.
  • There are two Sandbachs here: Bösensandbach at about 48.6155 N and Sandbach at about 48.6085 N, 800 m apart. I had measured to the wrong one. The scale figure computed from that pairing (1:10,300) was therefore wrong, and it was wrong in a way nothing internal to the calculation could have revealed.
  • Neuhofen, the sheet's title, sits at about 13.272 E, 48.634 N — just north of Kreiling. The sheet is named for a place near its northern edge.

20. The triangle that finally closed

First attempt at three matched points gave 0.31 m/px on one baseline and 0.51 on another — a 60% disagreement, which means a point is wrong, not that the scale is uncertain. Re-reading Kreiling's position off a drawn pixel grid rather than from a downsampled crop fixed it:

Kreiling -> Hahberg          5292 px   1727 m   0.3263 m/px   1:7708
Kreiling -> Boesensandbach   5427 px   1690 m   0.3113 m/px   1:7354
Hahberg  -> Boesensandbach   6801 px   2153 m   0.3166 m/px   1:7478

Three independent baselines agreeing to +-2.4%. A triangle that closes is evidence; two points that agree are not, because two points cannot disagree.

scale        about 1:7,500  (+-2.4% across baselines)
resolution   about 0.32 m per pixel
sheet        about 2.34 km square (neat line 7398 x 7370 px)

Neither stated scale survives. Not 1:25,000, the figure this session assumed for its sanity checks — off by a factor of 3.3. Not 1:5,000, the owner's figure — off by about 50%, though far closer than the assistant's. The map is its own authority on this and the measurement is repeatable from the numbers above.

21. What is NOT established, and why it stops here

The same three baselines imply the sheet is rotated about 8 degrees from north, consistently. That figure is not trustworthy yet: two of the three ground positions were read off the 1838 sheet, so a systematic bias in MY reading of that sheet would masquerade as rotation in exactly this way. An independent estimate from the Danube's bearing disagreed badly (about 49 degrees), which means at least one of the two is being measured wrongly. Recorded as unresolved.

The real blocker is stated plainly: georeferencing these sheets to the standard the 1838 sheet reached needs control good to +-20 m, and everything reachable from this session is +-150 to +-250 m — the 1838 sheet's own accuracy, or my ability to pick a hamlet centre on an engraving. Over a 2.3 km sheet that is a 10% error, and it is why the scale carries +-2.4% and the rotation carries nothing at all.

What would remove it, in order of usefulness: a modern reference layer (aerial imagery or vector data) that this network refuses; the series' sheet-corner specification, not found in the sources reached; or three or four points the instructor can confirm on the ground.

24. Control points from imagery, and the number that finally settled

Three settlements, each read off a 50 m grid on BOTH the sheet and Esri World Imagery. Both halves of every control point measured, neither assumed.

First attempt at the solve produced garbage — 117 degrees of rotation and 800 m residuals — because the sign convention for the sheet's downward-growing row axis was wrong in my own equations. Refitting as a general affine:

scale along sheet x    0.3196 m/px    1:7,550
scale along sheet y    0.3121 m/px    1:7,372
anisotropy             2.4%
sheet 'up' vs north    +2.7 deg       sheet +x vs east   -4.6 deg
sheet extent           about 2,340 m square

The anisotropy is the error bar. A real sheet is nearly similar — same scale both ways, axes at right angles. This one comes out that way to 2.4%, which is what three control points read at +-30 m over 2 km baselines can support, and it is the honest precision of everything above.

Two open questions closed:

  1. Rotation is about 0, +-4 degrees. The sheet is north-up. The "8 degrees" from section 21 was an artifact of control points read off the 1838 sheet, and the Danube-bearing estimate of 49 degrees was simply my bad measurement. Both are wrong and both are left in this log.
  2. Scale is 1:7,460 +-90. Not 1:5,000, not 1:25,000, and now pinned tightly enough that the remaining gap to any nominal figure is a question about the series, not about the measurement. For what it is worth the extent lands within 0.5% of 800 Bavarian Ruthen (2334.9 m), which would be a natural sheet quantum — offered as a lead, not a finding.

26. Sheet XXIII 52: a sheet with nothing on it to find

XXIV 54 was locatable because it showed the Danube, a road labelled Straubing one way and Passau the other, and a village name that a modern gazetteer knows. XXIII 52 has none of that. It is an interior sheet: one large forest block, a stream valley down the west side, and labels that are all farmsteads — "Unternoh", "Hinterschneid", "Eichelberg", a "…olfach" stream.

Three routes were tried and failed, in this order:

  1. Sheet-grid arithmetic. Assuming Roman = column and Arabic = row, one column west and two rows south of XXIV 54. Fetched imagery there: farmland with a sizeable village, against a sheet that is almost solid forest. Rejected on sight.
  2. The 1838 sheet as a name index. Searched its coverage around the Griesbach/Vilshofen district boundary — the sheet's own margin says "K. L. Griesbach und Vilshofen" — and found Ortenburg, Söldenau, Holzkirchen, Griesbach itself, Forst Steinkart. No Eichelberg, no Unternoh anywhere in 0.6 by 0.3 degrees. Evidence the sheet lies outside that coverage.
  3. Web search for the toponyms. Nothing. These names are too small to be indexed by general search.

What worked was a gazetteer query through the browser, the same channel that had already solved the imagery problem an hour earlier:

Zeitlarn, Landkreis Passau -> Zeitlarn, Vilshofen an der Donau  [48.6122, 13.1952]

Zeitlarn is written on sheet 52's top margin, and a name in the top margin lies just beyond the north edge. That put the north edge at about 48.611 — which is sheet XXIV 54's SOUTH edge, 48.609.

27. The numbering, which had been backwards all along

If the two sheets share an edge, they are ONE row apart, not two. And their longitudes differ by about 0.06 degrees, which is TWO sheet widths.

The Roman numeral is the ROW. The Arabic number is the COLUMN. XXIII is one row south of XXIV; 52 is two columns west of 54. Rows increase northward, columns eastward.

Predicted footprint from that rule, with no fitting at all:

13.18399, 48.58642  ->  13.21751, 48.60899

Fetched imagery there: stream valley down the west, one large forest block filling the centre and east, settlements strung along the valley. The same structure as the sheet, on sight. The rule is right.

One correction followed, from matching the Wolfach's meander apex between sheet and imagery: the arithmetic placement was 284 m too far east and 150 m too far north. Applied.
