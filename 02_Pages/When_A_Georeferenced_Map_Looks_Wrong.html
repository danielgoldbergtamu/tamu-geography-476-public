When a georeferenced map looks wrong

A real job, a real complaint, and the ten things that went wrong before it was right.

 

A real job, a real complaint, and the ten things that went wrong before it was right.

In September an outside researcher asked for four 19th-century maps of Passau, Bavaria to be georeferenced. The work was done with AI assistance and the files were delivered. Three weeks later the word came back: the maps were massively distorted.

They were not. Every pixel was at the correct longitude and latitude. Something else was wrong, and finding out what took a measurement rather than an argument. Along the way ten separate errors were made and caught. Six of them were confident numbers stated before anything had been checked.

This page is the record of all ten. You are reading it because you will make the same ten, and because the habit that catches them is the thing this course grades.

The complaint

What this shows Two panels of sheet N.O. XXIV 54 showing the same 2.4 kilometers of ground, each drawn on its own stored pixel grid, with a circle of 500 meter radius on the ground drawn in red over both. Left, before: the file stores 0.267 meter by 0.401 meter ground pixels, so the ring is an ellipse of 3,744 by 2,492 pixels, half again as wide as it is tall. Right, after: the file stores 0.320 meter square pixels and the ring is a circle of 3,125 by 3,125 pixels. This establishes that the complaint of massive distortion was a measurement of shape, not of position, and that reprojecting to a metric grid removed all of it.

The red ring is a circle on the ground with a radius of 500 meters. Both panels show the same 2.4 kilometers of ground. On the left it is drawn 3,744 pixels wide and 2,492 pixels tall. On the right it is drawn 3,125 by 3,125.

Two separate causes stack to produce the left panel.

The map was being drawn in a geographic coordinate system. A geographic system puts longitude and latitude on equal axes. At Passau's latitude of 48.6 degrees north, a degree of longitude covers only 0.66 as much ground as a degree of latitude. So everything is drawn about 1.5 times too wide. This is a display setting. It affects every geographic layer in the map, not just these maps, and nothing is wrong with the data.

Two of the three files were also stored on a grid that is 1.5 to 1 on the ground. They were warped to EPSG:4326 without an output grid being specified, so the software chose one pixel size for both axes. Pixels that are square in degrees are rectangles on the ground.

The fix was to reproject all three to a metric grid, ETRS89 / UTM zone 33N, with square meter pixels. The complaint was about shape. It was never about position.

Position was right the whole time

What this shows The georeferenced 1838 sheet with present-day town positions plotted on top of it as small crosses, each labeled. Every cross falls inside the drawn outline of the town it names. This establishes that the georeference places the sheet correctly on the Earth, which is the check that separates a wrong georeference from a correct one displayed badly.

This is the check that separates a wrong georeference from a correct one displayed badly, and it takes about four minutes. Plot a handful of places whose modern coordinates you can look up. If the crosses land on the right towns, position is fine and your problem is somewhere else.

Do this before you believe anyone who tells you a map is distorted, including yourself.

A mismatch is not always an error

What this shows A close view of the Danube and Ilz confluence at Passau, with the 1838 drawn channel and the present-day channel shown together. The two do not coincide: the 1838 bank sits tens of meters from the modern one. This establishes that a mismatch between an old map and a new one can be the river having moved rather than the georeference being wrong, and that the two causes look identical until you check a fixed landmark instead.

The 1838 bank does not sit on the modern bank. There are two reasons that can happen and they look identical on screen: the georeference is off, or the river moved in 188 years.

You tell them apart by checking something that does not move. A church, a bridge abutment, a road junction. If the fixed things line up and the river does not, the river moved.

Fitting one map to another inherits its error

What this shows Three panels in a row showing the two Positionsblatt sheets blended against the sheet they were fitted to, at the seam where they meet. Linework crosses the seam continuously in the middle panel and steps visibly in the outer two. This establishes that fitting a sheet to another sheet carries the first sheet's error into the second, so agreement at a seam is not evidence of accuracy.

Two of the four sheets had no graticule and no scale bar. Nothing on them could be georeferenced directly, so the 1838 sheet was used as the bridge: it names the same places and covers the same ground, so coordinates were read off it.

That works, and it carries the first sheet's error into the second. Agreement at a seam is not evidence of accuracy. It is evidence that two sheets are wrong in the same direction.

The three sheets are not one product and should not be reported as one.

Read these two again

Number 6 is the one to dwell on. The arithmetic was perfect. The input was the wrong village. An internal consistency check cannot catch a wrong input, and this one reported 2.4% internal consistency while being 800 meters off. A number that checks out against itself has told you nothing about whether it is true.

Number 10 is the one that should worry a GIS professional most. The output file had the right name, the right size, the right coordinate system and the right dimensions. Every automated check passed. It contained nothing at all. It was found by opening the raster and looking at it, which is the step that is easiest to skip and the only one that would have caught it.

Six of the ten were confident assumptions: numbers stated before anything was checked. Every one of the six was caught by measuring rather than by reasoning harder.

What this means for your own work

Four checks. Run them on anything you produce with AI assistance in this course.

  1. Check position against something you did not use to fit it. Control points agree with themselves by construction. A residual of zero on a three-point affine fit means the arithmetic worked, not that the map is right.
  2. Open the output and look at it. Read the pixels, render a thumbnail, draw a known distance on it. A correct header is not content.
  3. Measure the thing you assumed. Scale, rotation, units, which axis is which. Six of the ten errors above were assumptions that held until somebody measured.
  4. Separate what you fitted from what you inherited. Report accuracy per product, not per delivery, and say which numbers carry somebody else's error.

How to load the files in ArcGIS Pro

Use the files ending _utm33n.tif. Set the map's coordinate system before you add anything. That one setting causes the problem on its own.

  1. Insert then New Map.
  2. In the Contents pane, right-click Map, then Properties, then Coordinate Systems.
  3. Choose ETRS89 / UTM zone 33N. Search the filter box for 25833. Any projected system works. A geographic one does not.
  4. Map then Add Data, and add the _utm33n.tif files.
  5. When ArcGIS offers to build pyramids, say yes. These are large rasters and pyramids are what make panning usable.
  6. Add a basemap with Map then Basemap then Imagery, to check the fit.

To compare a sheet against modern imagery, select the raster layer, open the Raster Layer tab, and use Swipe, or set Transparency to about 50%.

In ArcMap 10.x the rule is the same. View then Data Frame Properties then Coordinate System, set a projected system, then add the rasters.

Do not add a raster to an empty map and let ArcGIS take its coordinate system from the first layer. If that first layer is geographic, the whole map becomes geographic and everything you add after it is drawn 1.5 times too wide.
