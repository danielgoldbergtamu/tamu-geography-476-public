The AI Review Log

Required with every graded submission in this course

 

What this is, in one paragraph

Every graded submission in this course arrives with an AI Review Log attached. It is a short, honest record of what you asked AI to do, what it said, what you did about it, and — most importantly — what you decided it was wrong about.

It is five fields. It should take about ten minutes if you actually did the work, and it is obvious when you did not.

Why this exists

Employers have stopped asking whether you use AI. They assume you do. What they are now trying to work out in interviews is whether you can direct it and judge what it gives back — because the person who accepts whatever the tool produces is worth very little, and is increasingly easy to replace with the tool.

This log is how that skill gets practiced and assessed. It is not a compliance form and it is not a confession. Nobody loses points for using AI here. You lose points for using it uncritically.

The five fields

The five required fields of the AI Review Log
1. Tools usedWhich tool and which model, e.g. TAMU AI Chat (Claude Sonnet) or Microsoft 365 Copilot. If you used more than one, say which did what.
2. Personas runWhich reviewer personas you used, and the actual prompt you gave each one. Not “I used the Hard Reviewer” — the prompt, so a reader can see what you asked for.
3. Top critiquesThe strongest two or three things each persona raised. Summarize them; do not paste the whole output.
4. Changes madeWhat you actually changed in response, and where. Section, paragraph, figure. Specific enough that I could go and look.
5. Critiques rejectedWhat you chose not to act on, and why the reviewer was wrong. This field carries the most weight on the rubric.

Field 5 is the one that is actually graded hard

Accepting every suggestion an AI makes is not review. It is transcription. A log where every critique was accepted tells me either that you did not read the output properly, or that you did not push the tool hard enough to produce anything worth rejecting.

AI is confidently wrong about GIS work fairly often — about projections, about what a statistic supports, about domain conventions it has only read about. Finding those moments and being able to say why you overruled it is the entire point. Two rejected critiques, with reasons, is the minimum on most assignments.

Why five fields and not seven

Three former intelligence officers — John O’Neil, Jim Lawler and Mike Mears, in A Spy’s Guide to Running AI (not included in the public copy — a third-party document, shared with this class under a permission that does not extend to redistribution) — propose a seven-field protocol for recording AI-assisted work, on the model of the debrief a case officer writes after meeting a source. Five of their seven are the five above. The two that are not are worth knowing about, because one of them is deliberately missing and the other is not.

Requirement — what question are we actually trying to answer? In an organization that field stops people prompting their way into work nobody asked for. Here the assignment prompt already is the requirement, and asking you to copy it back would be paperwork.

Review — what happened after we acted? Did the analysis hold up? They call this the step organizations most consistently skip, and they are right that it is where calibration happens. This log does not have it, because on a single submission there is nothing yet to review. You run it once, in the last studio of the semester, across every log you wrote — which of your rejections turned out to be wrong, and what that cost you.

A worked example

This is a real-shaped log for a Project Draft. Yours does not need to be longer than this.

Worked example of a completed AI Review Log
Tools usedTAMU AI Chat (Claude Sonnet 4.5) for review. First draft written by hand.
Personas runHard Reviewer — “You are a demanding reviewer for a geospatial journal. This is a draft section on livestock movement corridors in southern Mexico. Find the three weakest claims and say exactly why each is weak.”

Overclaim Detector — “Go through this line by line and flag every sentence that asserts more than the evidence in it supports. Quote the sentence and say what would be needed to justify it.”
Top critiques1. Hard Reviewer: I describe the road network as a ‘transmission corridor’ without showing any case data along it.
2. Hard Reviewer: no mention of how incomplete the market location data is.
3. Overclaim Detector: ‘cattle are routinely moved undetected’ — I have no source establishing routinely.
Changes madeRewrote the corridor paragraph (section 3.2) to say the network is a plausible pathway consistent with the detections, not a demonstrated one. Added a paragraph at the end of 3.3 on market data completeness and what is missing. Replaced ‘routinely’ with a specific statement of what the two documented cases show.
Critiques rejectedRejected: Overclaim Detector flagged my use of ‘suitable habitat’ as unsupported. It is wrong — that is a defined term from the environmental suitability literature with a stated threshold, which I cite. It read a technical term as a loose claim.

Rejected: Hard Reviewer wanted a formal uncertainty quantification. With two confirmed detections there is nothing to quantify; a false precision would be worse than the qualitative statement I made. I say so explicitly in the text now.

How to submit it

  • Put it at the end of the same file as your submission, under a heading AI Review Log. A separate file is fine too, but one file is easier for both of us.
  • It does not count toward the assignment’s length target. Say what you need to say and stop.
  • If you genuinely used no AI on an assignment, say that and say why. It is a legitimate answer that occasionally has a good reason behind it — but it needs the reason.

The habit this is really building

In about two years someone will hand you AI output and ask whether it is any good. Everyone in the room will be able to generate it. Far fewer will be able to say, with reasons, which parts of it are wrong. That is what this log trains, and it is why it is attached to every single thing you submit rather than taught once and forgotten.

Where the prompts live

Every persona named on an assignment has a ready-made prompt in the AI Prompt Library, and the AI Reviewer Persona Catalog explains what each one is for. Adapt them — name your dataset, your component, your actual constraint. A persona given no context returns generic advice, and generic advice is what you already had.
