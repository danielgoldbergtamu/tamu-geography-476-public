What the data turned out to be

Every source that was tried, including the ones that gave nothing. A source that yielded nothing is a finding, not a gap in the record.

What does not exist

  • A scale bar or graticule on either 19th-century sheet. Their scale had to be MEASURED from matched control, and came out at 1:7,460 — neither the 1:25,000 the assistant assumed for its sanity checks nor the 1:5,000 the owner reports.
  • Any documentation of this sheet series' grid, in any source reached. The numbering was decoded from two sheets and one gazetteer lookup instead.
  • Reachable modern imagery from either shell. Ten hosts probed on one occasion and four more later; all refused. The browser was the only route.

Where the sources disagreed

Reported rather than resolved. When two sources disagree and neither is obviously wrong, the disagreement is part of the result.

  • What is the scale of the two Positionsblatt sheets?
  • The owner reports 1:5,000
  • The assistant assumed 1:25,000 for its sanity checks
  • Measurement from three matched baselines gives 1:7,460 +-90
  • Which Munich meridian does the 1838 sheet's graticule use?
  • The observatory value 11 deg 36' 32.24" puts the sheet 600 m east of modern coordinates
  • The check points imply 11 deg 36' 02.79", which may instead be absorbing a datum shift

What was NOT built, and why

Read this section first if you read nothing else. The deliverable that was refused is the one you would most likely have produced.

  • Drawing a scale bar on the 1838 figures. The JPEG carries no extent and the georeferenced TIFF was on a blocked host. A plausible-looking scale bar is a measurement claim, and an invented measurement is worse than a missing one.
  • Built instead: A stated "what this figure does not have" line on the figure.
  • Fitting scale and rotation for sheet XXIII 52. Two matched features can move a sheet; they cannot fit its scale or its rotation. Fitting four parameters to two points produces numbers with no evidence behind them and no way to tell.
  • Built instead: The grid's scale and north-up assumption kept, the sheet shifted, and the weaker accuracy stated in the file, the log and the folder's own layout entry.
  • Control points B and C on sheet XXIII 52. The paired crops did not overlap — the sheet showed forest where the imagery showed a village. Choosing which building "must" be the match is how a confident wrong control point is made.
  • Built instead: One control point, and a stated accuracy that reflects it.

What no check would have caught

The rules and gates this course runs on, and why each one would have passed the version that was wrong.

  • any checker in this project — none of them open a JPEG or compare two frames
  • residuals, on a 3-point affine: it is exact by construction and reports zero
  • looking at the finished overlay, where a 600 m datum shift is invisible without a reference

What went wrong while building it

  • A similarity fit returned 117 degrees of rotation and 800 m residuals. The sign convention for the sheet's downward-growing row axis was wrong in my own equations. Caught by The answer being absurd. Nothing flagged it..
  • Measured the wrong village — Sandbach instead of Bösensandbach, 800 m apart. Two places with nearly the same name, and I transcribed the red script wrong. Caught by The 1838 sheet, which names both. It produced a confident, wrong scale of 1:10,300 first..
  • Spent an hour treating "every imagery host is blocked" as settled. A true statement about the shells, carried forward as a statement about the job. Caught by The instructor..

What to take from it

  • A graticule is worth an hour of searching. Sheet 65 had one and fits to 1.5 px; the sheets without one cost five times the effort for a tenth of the accuracy.
  • Coordinates are meaningless without a datum. The 1838 sheet is internally precise to 6 m and sits 600 m east of WGS84, because its graticule is in a 19th-century Bavarian astronomic datum.
  • Redundancy catches what checkers cannot. 387 dpi against 390 dpi, a triangle that closes, 2.4% anisotropy — each is a second measurement of a thing already measured, and each caught a real error.
  • An accuracy figure produced by looking is not an accuracy figure. Draw the scale bar, then measure against it.
  • The map is the authority on its own scale. Two humans reported two different scales and both were wrong; the sheet settled it.
  • A gazetteer answers confidently whether or not it is right. "Aigenberg" returned a mountain 200 km away in the same session that "Zeitlarn" cracked the sheet grid.
  • Say which product someone is holding. Two warped copies of the same sheet, one faithful to the map and one shifted to modern coordinates, are different claims about the world.

What this means for the job you are about to apply for

A junior analyst asked to "georeference this old map" would produce a file that opens in the right country and looks convincing. Everything that made today's work worth anything was refusal and redundancy: refusing the scale bar, refusing to fit four parameters to two points, refusing the third control point that would not resolve, and measuring twice by different means every time a number mattered. The georeferencing itself is a solved problem with a known button. Knowing what the output is worth is not.
