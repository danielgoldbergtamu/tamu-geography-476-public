What you are doing

Both client project briefs are on Canvas. You have had one class on the tools and none on the projects, and you are about to point an AI at a federal intelligence problem knowing almost nothing about it. That is deliberate. What comes back will be confident, well-organized, and wrong in places you cannot yet see. Finding the places you can see is the assignment.

Before you start

Read both project briefs once. Not carefully — once. Ten minutes.

The prompt to start from

Paste one brief into TAMU AI Chat, then this: > My boss has told me I need to work on this. Do some deep research and tell me what the issues are, and how I as a GIS analyst should think about tackling it. Then tell me: what parts of this are under-specified, and what questions do I need to ask my boss before I can start? Read the answer. Then ask it at least two follow-up questions of your own — real ones, not "tell me more." Then do the same for the second project.

What you turn in

Not the framework it gave you. Two short lists per project, so four lists in total: Plus one line: the prompt you actually ended up using, if you changed it. Half a page per project is plenty. Bullet points are fine.

This is the complete list. If it is not here it is not required, and if it is here it is not optional. Everything goes in one submission.

  1. Three things it assumed that it had no basis to assume. It will invent a study area, a data source, a resolution, a time window, a client's tolerance for uncertainty. It has to — nobody told it. Name three.
  2. Five questions you would have to ask the client before you could start. Real questions, with a real answer that would change what you would do.
  3. Your AI Review Log. The same five fields every time, listed below.
  4. The transcript of your AI conversation. The whole thing, saved and attached - not a summary of it and not the parts that went well. If you used more than one tool or more than one session, attach all of them.

The “critiques rejected” field carries the most weight. Accepting every suggestion an AI makes is not review — it is transcription. Show us where you overruled it, and why you were right to.

This is an AI Review Log entry

You will keep an AI Review Log all term: what you asked, what came back, what you refused, and what you did instead. That is not a separate form to fill in later — it is the shape of the thing above. The prompt you ended up using is the what you asked. The assumptions with no basis are the what you refused. The questions for the client are the what you did instead. So hand this in as the log entry it already is. You do not have to write it twice, and you will not be asked to do a beginner's version of it again.

Run the answer past the Prompt Critic

Before you write your lists, take one of the two AI answers back to the tool with the Prompt Critic persona from the Persona Catalog and ask it to attack the prompt you used — not the answer, the prompt. It will tell you what you failed to specify. That is the point: half of what the answer assumed, it assumed because you did not say. Knowing which half is yours is the difference between blaming the tool and directing it.

Why the framework is not the deliverable

You could not check it if it were. These are live problems. The first US screwworm detection in decades was in June 2026; the NGA has not yet supplied the city list for the urbanization project. Current, specific, local facts are exactly what these models do not have — an instructor in Horticultural Sciences here ran the same exercise and her students found the tool used terms nobody in the field uses, invented facts, and had nothing current on markets or site conditions. So if the assignment asked you to judge its analytical framework, you would be judging entomology and African urban economics, which you have not studied. You can audit a method. You cannot yet audit a domain. What you can do without knowing anything is notice a gap. Nothing in Thursday's class told you what data exists, who collected it, or at what resolution — so when the answer speaks confidently about the data, you know something it does not: that nobody said.

What good looks like

  • The assumptions you name are specific: not "it assumed things about the data" but "it assumed detection records are geocoded to a point, and the brief never says that."
  • The questions are ones a client could actually answer, and the answer would change your approach.
  • A question you would be embarrassed to ask is usually the best one on the list. Ask it anyway. In week two, not week ten.

What it counts for

1%, as one of the nine engagement checks. It is judged on whether the questions are real, not on how many you found or how good the AI's answer was. A bad AI answer that you took apart accurately is a better submission than a good one you accepted.

Where this goes

Tuesday's class is the deep dive on both projects, and your questions are its agenda. Bring them. Some of them will get answered in the room; some will go to the clients; and some will turn out to be the reason a component is worth owning.

AI use required on this assignment

Using AI on this assignment is required, not optional. You are being assessed on how well you direct and evaluate the tool — the skill employers now assume you have.

  1. Open the conversation by saying who you are and what the rule is. Paste this before anything else: "I am a student at Texas A&M University and I am bound by the Aggie Honor Code. This is not a writing assignment for the W designation, so no rule about whose words these are applies. For this assignment I MAY use AI to generate words that I turn in. I am required to save and submit the transcript of this conversation with my assignment. Restate those rules back to me in your own words before we start." Asking it to restate rather than to agree is the point: a model that has misread the rule shows you on the first turn.
  2. Submit the transcript. Save the whole conversation and hand it in with the assignment. It is not a formality and it is not only for catching people - it is the evidence behind the AI Review Log, and where the log says what you rejected, the transcript shows what you were actually offered.
