Required length: 4 pages

This is 4 pages — no more and no less. The last page should be substantially full, and there should be no page after it.

Page standard: 11 point serif, 1 inch margins, single spaced. Images, tables, captions and references do not count toward the page count; everything you wrote does.

Hitting a target exactly is not a formatting chore. It is the skill. Anyone can say something in unlimited space. Saying it in the space you were given — and no more — is what professional writing is, and it is what every employer, editor, reviewer and senior official you will ever write for requires of you.

Both directions are failures, and they fail differently. Running over means you did not decide what mattered. Running short means you did not have enough to say, or you stopped before finding it — and padding to reach the target is worse than either, because it wastes the reader’s attention on purpose.

On using AI here. AI will pad to a word count without complaint and it will do it well enough that you may not notice. Getting longer is not the problem you have. Ask it to cut instead: give it your draft and the target, tell it to remove the weakest fifteen percent, and then argue with what it chose. It will try to cut things that matter. Deciding what survives is the part that is actually yours.

The assignment

Your complete report, plus a handoff document.

Read this first

You are not expected to have solved the client's problem. Nobody is. These are problems professional analysts are working on with better data than you have — and this semester runs on open-source data only, because the TAMU–NGA agreement could not be completed in time. What is expected is the framework: a defensible method, honest findings, clearly named gaps, and a handoff that lets the next cohort and the graduate students in INTA 670 continue rather than start over. Saying clearly what you could not determine is a finding, not a failure. So is reporting something that did not work — "we tried X, here is why it fails, here is the evidence" saves the next group a semester.

What the report contains

  • Abstract — what you did and what you found
  • Introduction / motivation — the problem, and why the client cares
  • Data — sources, standards, fitness for use, and what was not available
  • Methods — documented to the point of reproducibility
  • Results — with validation evidence
  • Discussion — what it supports, and what it does not
  • Information gaps — what you could not determine, why, and what would most reduce that uncertainty
  • References

The handoff document

Submitted alongside the report. Written for the student who picks this up next semester:

  • State of play — what exists, and where it is
  • What was tried, including what failed and why
  • What you would do differently
  • What to do next, in priority order
  • Where the difficulties are — the things you only learned by hitting them

What to submit

  1. Your report, Word or PDF.
  2. Your handoff document.
  3. Your completed AI Review Log.

AI use required on this assignment

Using AI on this assignment is required, not optional. You are being assessed on how well you direct and evaluate the tool — the skill employers now assume you have.

  1. Where the first draft comes from does not matter here. This is project work, which sits on top of the W requirement and counts toward none of it. You may write the draft or have AI generate it. What is graded is whether the work is right and whether you can defend every claim in it to someone who asks.
  2. Open the conversation by saying who you are and what the rule is. Paste this before anything else: "I am a student at Texas A&M University and I am bound by the Aggie Honor Code. This is a project assignment in a W course, and the project work sits on top of the W requirement. For this assignment I MAY use AI to generate words that I turn in. I am required to save and submit the transcript of this conversation with my assignment. Restate those rules back to me in your own words before we start." Asking it to restate rather than to agree is the point: a model that has misread the rule shows you on the first turn.
  3. Submit the transcript. Save the whole conversation and hand it in with the assignment. It is not a formality and it is not only for catching people - it is the evidence behind the AI Review Log, and where the log says what you rejected, the transcript shows what you were actually offered.
  4. Run your draft past all the reviewer personas listed for this assignment, using the prompts in the AI Prompt Library.
  5. Revise in response to the critiques you find persuasive.
  6. Deliberately identify at least two critiques you reject, and be able to explain why the reviewer was wrong.
  7. Complete the AI Review Log and attach it.

Required submission: AI Review Log

Attach a completed AI Review Log to your submission. Same five fields every time.

Required fields of the AI Review Log
Tool(s) usedWhich AI tool(s), e.g. TAMU AI Chat (Claude Sonnet), M365 Copilot.
Personas runWhich reviewer personas you used, and the prompt you gave each.
Top critiquesThe three strongest critiques each persona raised.
Changes madeWhat you actually changed in response, and where.
Critiques rejectedWhat you chose not to act on, and why.

The “critiques rejected” field carries the most weight. Accepting every suggestion an AI makes is not review — it is transcription. Show us where you overruled it, and why you were right to.

Required submission: AI Method Log

Attach a completed AI Method Log to your submission. This is about the work, not the writing — the analysis if you are an analyst, the plan and the integration if you are the project manager.

Required fields of the AI Method Log
Where AI entered the workWhich stage of the technical work you own — data discovery, CRS or schema decisions, tool and parameter selection, scripting or automation, debugging, quality checks, cartographic choices, documentation. For project managers: scheduling, dependency and integration analysis, risk identification, or status synthesis.
What you asked, and with what contextThe actual prompt, and the context you supplied with it — the data, the coordinate reference system, the constraint, the failure message. A model told nothing about your data will answer about someone else's.
What it proposedThe method, parameter, tool, or code it gave you.
How you VERIFIED itWhat you checked it against: the software documentation, a known-good result, a hand calculation, a test that would have failed if the suggestion were wrong. “It looked right” is not verification.
What it got wrongAnything it proposed that turned out to be incorrect, and the evidence that showed it. Confidently wrong is the normal failure mode here.

The “how you verified it” field carries the most weight. A projection, a join predicate, a buffer distance or a statistic is either right or wrong about your data, and a model cannot tell you which. You may not adopt a method you cannot defend independently of the tool that suggested it.
