Required length: 3 pages

This is 3 pages — no more and no less. The last page should be substantially full, and there should be no page after it.

Page standard: 11 point serif, 1 inch margins, single spaced. Images, tables, captions and references do not count toward the page count; everything you wrote does.

Hitting a target exactly is not a formatting chore. It is the skill. Anyone can say something in unlimited space. Saying it in the space you were given — and no more — is what professional writing is, and it is what every employer, editor, reviewer and senior official you will ever write for requires of you.

Both directions are failures, and they fail differently. Running over means you did not decide what mattered. Running short means you did not have enough to say, or you stopped before finding it — and padding to reach the target is worse than either, because it wastes the reader’s attention on purpose.

On using AI here. AI will pad to a word count without complaint and it will do it well enough that you may not notice. Getting longer is not the problem you have. Ask it to cut instead: give it your draft and the target, tell it to remove the weakest fifteen percent, and then argue with what it chose. It will try to cut things that matter. Deciding what survives is the part that is actually yours.

The assignment

An essay analyzing a real ethical dilemma facing a geospatial professional, stating what you consider the ethical course of action, and arguing for it. You are not given the answer, and there is no answer key. Reasonable professionals disagree about these cases — what is assessed is the quality of your reasoning, not whether you land where I would.

Where your dilemma comes from

Write about a dilemma from your own project. By now you have claimed a component, gone looking for data and checked what you are licensed to do with it, and run a pilot. Ethical friction has already happened to you — in the license you were not sure covered your use, in the thing your data could show about people who never agreed to be in it, in the conclusion you could not quite support but wanted to. Each project page carries a list of real dilemmas for that project. Use one, or use something you hit yourself. If you have a better case that is not about your project, use it. Some of you will have one — from a job, an internship, another class, something you read that will not leave you alone. To use it, add one short paragraph at the top of your draft saying what the case is and why it is stronger for you than a dilemma from your project. I will almost always say yes. The paragraph exists to make you compare the two, not to make you ask permission. You will never be stuck for a topic. Your project is always available as the fallback.

What makes this work

  • The dilemma is stated precisely: who is affected, what is at stake, what the competing obligations actually are.
  • You take a position. An essay that surveys the considerations and declines to conclude has not done the assignment.
  • You engage a recognized professional framework — the GISCI Code of Ethics, the URISA GIS Code of Ethics, or an equivalent — rather than gesturing at "ethics" in general.
  • You address the strongest argument against your position, and say why it does not change your mind. If it partly does, say that instead.
  • You distinguish what is illegal, what is against professional standards, and what is merely unwise. They are not the same category and the difference usually matters.

A caution specific to this assignment

AI tools are noticeably bad at ethical reasoning in a way that is easy to miss: they tend to produce balanced, agreeable prose that surveys every side and commits to none. That is exactly the failure mode this assignment is grading against. Use AI to stress-test your position. Do not let it talk you out of having one. There is a second reason this assignment works the way it does. A famous case — the Cambridge Analytica scandal, the Strava heatmap — has a large body of commentary written about it already, and an AI tool can reproduce that commentary fluently because it has read all of it. Your project dilemma has no commentary written about it. Nobody has published on what you should do about the license on your dataset. That means the reasoning has to be yours, and it also means the reviewer personas become far more useful — the Opposing Advocate has to think about your situation rather than retrieve a consensus about someone else's.

The trap in writing about your own work

You made a decision. It is very easy to fill the whole three pages explaining why it was the right one. That is not the assignment, and the rubric is built to catch it. The counterargument criterion asks you to construct the case that runs against your own interest — that what you did, or are about to do, was wrong — and to put it in its strongest form rather than a version you can knock over. If you cannot build that case, you have not examined the decision; you have defended it. This is the single most useful thing this assignment can teach you, and it is why the Opposing Advocate persona is required here.

Peer review required — AI required, used critically

You will review 2 classmate submission(s) through Canvas.

Reviews open: Thursday 1 October, at the deadline  ·  Reviews due: Monday 5 October, 11:59 PM

Focus your review on:

  • Is there a clear position, or does the essay hedge?
  • Is the dilemma stated precisely enough that a reader knows what is actually at stake?
  • Is a real professional framework used, or is "ethics" invoked in the abstract?
  • Is the strongest counterargument faced honestly, or set up to be knocked down?
  • If it is about their own project: does the essay examine the decision, or defend it? Would the author of this essay have been able to change their mind?

AI is required for this review — in this order:

  1. Read it yourself first. No AI. Write down two or three things you think are strong and two or three you think are weak. Do this before you open any AI tool — and paste that list at the top of your review, unchanged. It is the only proof that you formed a view of your own, and it is the first thing read.
  2. Now run the AI reviewer personas on the draft, using the peer-review prompts in the AI Prompt Library.
  3. Compare. What did AI catch that you missed? What did you catch that AI missed? Where is AI simply wrong about this draft?
  4. Write the review in your own voice, marking which points came from your own reading, which from AI, and where you overruled AI.

Why that order. If you read the AI critique first you will anchor to it, and you will lose the ability to judge whether it is any good. Forming your own view first is what makes you a reviewer rather than a relay.

How this is graded. Not on how much you wrote. On what you added beyond what the AI already said, and on where you correctly judged the AI to be wrong. A review that is a lightly edited AI output earns very little.

Professional note: you are putting a classmate’s unpublished work into a tool. Use TAMU AI Chat or Copilot, which are university-protected, and treat their draft the way you would want yours treated.
